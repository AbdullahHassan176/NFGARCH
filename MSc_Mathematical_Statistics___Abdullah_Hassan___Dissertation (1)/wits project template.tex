% Abstract

\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}
\noindent
This dissertation examines whether flexible, data-driven innovation distributions can improve the empirical realism and forecasting accuracy of GARCH-family volatility models while preserving their variance dynamics by introducing a novel two-stage Normalising Flow-GARCH ("NF-GARCH") framework. In the first stage, standard GARCH variants are estimated on return series to extract standardised residuals. In the second stage, a Masked Autoregressive Flow ("MAF") is trained on these residuals to learn a non-parametric innovation distribution, which is subsequently re-injected into the unchanged GARCH recursion for simulation and forecasting.\\

The empirical analysis considers 13 daily financial time series in full pipeline mode: six foreign exchange ("FX") pairs and seven equity prices. NF-GARCH improves out-of-sample forecast accuracy for some specifications (notably gjrGARCH and sGARCH with skewed-$t$ innovations), with statistical significance confirmed by Wilcoxon tests for those two. Distributional diagnostics show lower Kolmogorov--Smirnov and Wasserstein distances for NF-augmented models such as gjrGARCH.\\

Overall, NF‑GARCH retains the interpretability of classical volatility models while improving residual distributional realism and, for some specifications (gjrGARCH and sGARCH with skewed-$t$), return-forecast accuracy. Limitations include unconditional innovation modelling, eGARCH instability under certain configurations, and higher computational cost. Future work should explore conditional and multivariate NF‑GARCH extensions to better capture regime‑dependent asymmetry and cross‑asset dependence.\\


\textbf{Keywords:} Normalising Flows, GARCH, EGARCH, TGARCH, sGARCH, gjrGARCH, NF-GARCH, Financial Volatility, Residual Modelling, Heavy Tails, Financial Econometrics, Stylized Facts, Stress Testing\\

\chapter{Introduction}

\section{Background}
\noindent
Modelling financial time series, including exchange rates and equity returns, poses significant challenges due to the complexity and non-linear dynamics of financial markets. Stylized facts such as volatility clustering, leverage effects, heavy tails, and serial autocorrelation frequently violate the assumptions of classical linear models. Consequently, econometricians have adopted models from the ARCH family to address the conditional heteroskedasticity observed in asset returns. The Generalised Autoregressive Conditional Heteroskedasticity model remains a foundational tool for modelling time-varying volatility.\\

GARCH and its extensions, including EGARCH \parencite{nelson1991conditional}, TGARCH \parencite{zakoian1994threshold}, and GJR-GARCH \parencite{glosten1993relation}, offer increasingly flexible structures for modelling asymmetric volatility responses and persistence. These models generally assume that standardised residuals follow a fixed parametric distribution, most commonly Gaussian, Student-t, or Generalised Error Distribution ("GED"). However, these assumptions are frequently violated in empirical data, where return distributions may display skewness, excess kurtosis, or multi-modality that standard parametric families cannot capture \textcite{ederington2005forecasting}. While volatility dynamics in financial time series are now well captured by the GARCH family and its asymmetric extensions, the specification of the innovation distribution remains largely parametric and restrictive. In practice, standard Gaussian or symmetric heavy-tailed assumptions are often imposed for tractability, despite mounting evidence that return innovations exhibit complex, asset-dependent distributional features that are not easily captured by fixed parametric families. This paper focuses specifically on this second source of misspecification—innovation distributions—while retaining a conventional and interpretable volatility recursion.\\

Alternative approaches to innovation modelling include skewed parametric distributions (e.g., skewed-t or GED), semi- and non-parametric ARCH specifications, and adversarial generative models such as Generative Adversarial Networks. While these methods offer varying degrees of flexibility, they typically trade off either interpretability, likelihood tractability, or stability. Normalising Flows occupy a distinctive middle ground: they provide highly flexible density representations while retaining exact likelihoods, controlled tail behaviour, and stable maximum-likelihood estimation, making them particularly suitable for risk-focused econometric settings. This is made possible by Normalising Flows' ability to model highly expressive probability distributions via a sequence of invertible, differentiable transformations applied to a simple base distribution, while maintaining exact likelihood evaluation and efficient sampling (Rezende and Mohamed, 2015; Papamakarios et al., 2021). Despite requiring additional architectural decisions and incurring higher computational costs, their superior expressiveness and tractable likelihoods render them more suitable than alternative flexible approaches for modelling innovation distributions in GARCH-type models. This comparative assessment, therefore, justifies the adoption of Normalising Flows in the present study.\\

The choice of innovation distribution in GARCH models has long been recognised as critical to forecasting accuracy and risk measurement. Early GARCH specifications assumed Gaussian innovations \parencite{bollerslev1986generalised}, but this assumption was quickly challenged by empirical evidence of heavy tails and skewness in financial returns. \textcite{bollerslev1987conditionally} introduced Student-$t$ innovations to capture excess kurtosis, significantly improving tail-risk estimation and Value-at-Risk forecasting. \textcite{nelson1991conditional} further extended this work by demonstrating that leverage effects—asymmetric volatility responses to positive versus negative shocks—could not be adequately captured by symmetric innovation distributions alone, motivating the development of EGARCH.\\

Subsequent research explored increasingly flexible parametric families. \textcite{fernandez1998multivariate} proposed skewed distributions to jointly model asymmetry and heavy tails. \textcite{hansen1994autoregressive} demonstrated that even small misspecifications in the innovation density can propagate into substantial forecast errors, particularly for multi-step-ahead predictions. Despite these advances, parametric approaches impose restrictive functional forms that may not adequately capture complex, asset-specific distributional features such as multi-modality or state-dependent asymmetry.\\

Recognition of parametric limitations motivated the development of semi-parametric and non-parametric GARCH specifications. \textcite{engle1993measuring} introduced kernel-based density estimation for standardised GARCH residuals, demonstrating improved tail estimation relative to parametric benchmarks. \textcite{hansen2004nonparametric} developed nonparametric conditional density estimation methods that offer flexibility without strong parametric assumptions. However, these approaches often suffer from curse-of-dimensionality issues \parencite{silverman1986density}, require extensive hyperparameter tuning, or lack the tractable likelihood evaluation necessary for risk management applications.\\

Normalising Flows \parencite{rezende2015variational, dinh2016density} address many limitations of alternative generative models by providing exact likelihood evaluation through invertible transformations. \textcite{papamakarios2021normalising} provides a comprehensive survey, emphasising their advantages for density estimation: exact likelihoods, stable training via maximum likelihood, and efficient sampling.\\

A growing body of research explores hybrid models that combine econometric structure with machine learning flexibility. For volatility modelling specifically, \textcite{kim2018forecasting} integrated GARCH with LSTM networks, demonstrating improved forecasting performance. \textcite{hossain2008comparison} compared neural networks with GARCH models in financial time series prediction, finding trade-offs between flexibility and interpretability. Critically, \textcite{harvey2022machine} argue that successful hybrid models must satisfy three criteria: (1) preserve interpretability of economic parameters, (2) maintain testable statistical properties, and (3) demonstrate robust out-of-sample performance. The two-stage NF-GARCH framework proposed in this dissertation is explicitly designed to meet these criteria by isolating innovation flexibility from volatility dynamics, thereby enabling rigorous evaluation of the marginal contribution of flexible innovation modelling.\\

Despite extensive research on both GARCH innovation specifications and normalising flows, a significant gap remains: there is limited empirical evidence on whether flexible, nonparametric innovation distributions—specifically those learned via normalising flows—provide systematic improvements in forecasting accuracy and distributional realism when integrated into classical GARCH frameworks \emph{without modifying the volatility recursion}.\\

Previous hybrid approaches either: (1) alter both volatility dynamics and innovation distributions simultaneously \parencite{seitz2022garchnf}, making it impossible to isolate the contribution of innovation flexibility; (2) sacrifice interpretability by replacing GARCH structures entirely with black-box neural networks \parencite{bao2017deep}; or (3) remain purely parametric, limiting their ability to capture complex, asset-specific distributional features \parencite{hansen1994autoregressive}.\\

This dissertation addresses this gap by proposing a \emph{modular} two-stage NF-GARCH framework that preserves classical volatility dynamics while replacing parametric innovation assumptions with flow-learned densities. This design enables direct assessment of whether innovation flexibility alone—independent of changes to variance recursion—can meaningfully improve forecasting, risk measurement, and distributional realism across asset classes.\\

This study evaluates a hybrid approach, termed \emph{NF-GARCH}, which retains the standard GARCH variance recursion while replacing the residual distribution with a learned Normalising Flow trained on standardised residuals. The primary objective is to relax the rigid distributional assumptions imposed on GARCH residuals by substituting the traditional noise term with samples drawn from a trained Normalising Flow. This approach yields a semi-parametric volatility model that preserves the structure of classical GARCH while introducing distributional flexibility in the innovations. The methodology involves fitting a GARCH-type model to observed returns, extracting standardised residuals, training a Normalising Flow to model the empirical distribution of these residuals, and subsequently re-simulating or forecasting returns using samples from the flow rather than assuming a fixed parametric noise law.\\ 

This approach enables the model to more accurately capture empirical features of return distributions, including skewness, fat tails, and dynamic changes in distributional shape over time. Four classical GARCH variants - Standard GARCH ("SGARCH"), Exponential GARCH ("EGARCH"), Threshold GARCH ("TGARCH"), and Glosten–Jagannathan–Runkle GARCH ("GJR-GARCH") - are evaluated alongside their respective NF-augmented versions across 13 assets in full pipeline mode: six exchange rate and seven equity return series. Model evaluation incorporates traditional loss functions such as Mean Squared Error ("MSE"), Mean Absolute Error ("MAE"), and log-likelihood \parencite{amisano2007comparing}, as well as stylized diagnostics including tail index estimates \parencite{hill1975simple}, Kolmogorov-Smirnov ("KS") distance \parencite{theis2015note}, and Value-at-Risk backtesting \parencite{kupiec1995techniques, christoffersen1998evaluating}.\\

\subsubsection{Design choice and relation to prior work:}
A two-stage innovation-modelling strategy is employed to separate volatility estimation from distributional learning. In the initial stage, a standard GARCH-family model is fitted under conventional parametric assumptions to estimate the time-varying volatility dynamics of the return series. After estimating the volatility process, the resulting standardised residuals, which represent return shocks after accounting for conditional volatility, are extracted.\\

In the second stage, a Normalising Flow model is trained on the standardised residuals to learn a flexible, data-driven representation of the innovation distribution. Samples generated from the trained flow are subsequently rescaled by the previously estimated volatility process and utilised for forecasting, simulation, and risk analysis. Notably, the volatility recursion remains unchanged throughout this procedure.\\

This modular approach contrasts with end-to-end “flow-after-scaling” frameworks, such as those proposed by \textcite{seitz2022garchnf}, where the Normalising Flow is applied directly to volatility-scaled returns and both the flow and volatility parameters are jointly optimised within a single likelihood function. Although such end-to-end models enable the conditional return distribution to adapt explicitly with the volatility state, they also entangle volatility dynamics with distributional learning, thereby increasing estimation complexity and reducing transparency.\\

In contrast, the two-stage framework preserves the interpretability and robustness of classical GARCH volatility modelling while isolating the effect of flexible innovation distributions. The principal trade-off is that the learned innovation distribution remains invariant to the volatility state, except for linear rescaling. This design choice is intentional, as it enables a direct assessment of whether enhancing innovation modelling alone, without modifying the volatility recursion.\\

\section{Problem Statement and Research Question}
\noindent
This dissertation investigates whether integrating Normalising Flows into the innovation law of GARCH-family models enhances their ability to capture the empirical characteristics of financial returns and improves out-of-sample performance across asset classes. Specifically, the study asks:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Main Research Question:} 
To what extent can the integration of Normalising Flows into the innovation process of GARCH-family models improve forecasting accuracy, distributional realism, and risk calibration, while preserving the stylized facts of exchange rate and equity return series?
\end{quote}

\section{Aims and Objectives}

\subsection{Aims}
\noindent 
The primary aim of this research is to evaluate whether integrating Normalising Flows into the innovation law of GARCH-family models enhances their empirical realism and predictive performance for exchange rate and equity returns. Specifically, the study seeks to:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Identify and quantify the key empirical properties (stylized facts) of financial return series that volatility models should reproduce.
    \item Evaluate the ability of standard GARCH-family models—sGARCH, EGARCH, TGARCH, and GJR-GARCH—to capture these stylized facts.
    \item Develop and implement Normalising Flow-enhanced GARCH ("NF-GARCH") models by replacing the fixed parametric innovation law with a learned residual distribution.
    \item Assess whether NF integration improves:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Distributional realism (e.g., heavy tails, skewness, kurtosis)
        \item Stylized fact preservation (e.g., volatility clustering, leverage effect)
        \item Forecasting accuracy and robustness under extreme market conditions
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Objectives}
\noindent 
The specific objectives of the study are to:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Conduct a comprehensive literature review covering:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Stylized facts in financial time series
        \item GARCH-family volatility models
        \item Applications of Normalising Flows in time-series and volatility modelling
    \end{itemize}

    \item Fit and evaluate the following classical volatility models on real-world FX and equity return data.

    \item Extract standardised residuals from each fitted model and examine their empirical distributional properties (e.g., QQ-plots, tail index, KS distance).

    \item Train Normalising Flows (e.g., MAF) on these residuals to model flexible, non-Gaussian innovation distributions.

    \item Construct NF-augmented GARCH variants (NF-sGARCH, NF-EGARCH, etc.) by reinjecting flow-generated innovations into the volatility recursion and generating forecasts.

    \item Compare the models with and without the NF-augmented innovations using:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Forecasting metrics:} MSE, MAE, and out-of-sample log-likelihood
        \item \textbf{Distributional metrics:} KS statistic, Wasserstein distance, tail index, skewness, and kurtosis
        \item \textbf{Stylized-fact diagnostics:} volatility clustering, asymmetry, persistence
        \item \textbf{Stress-test robustness:} performance under crisis periods (e.g., 2008 GFC, 2020 COVID-19)
    \end{itemize}

    \item Analyse trade-offs in model complexity and interpretability by:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Comparing computational cost versus empirical improvement
        \item Discussing interpretability, stability, and practical deployment considerations
    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\section{Contributions of This Dissertation}
\label{sec:contributions}

\noindent
This dissertation makes three principal contributions to the literature on volatility modelling, innovation specification, and hybrid econometric-machine learning methods:\\

We introduce a principled two-stage NF-GARCH architecture that separates volatility estimation from innovation density learning. Unlike end-to-end approaches \parencite{seitz2022garchnf}, our design preserves parameter identifiability, maintains interpretability of volatility dynamics, and enables rigorous evaluation of the marginal contribution of flexible innovation modelling. This modularity makes the framework suitable for regulated financial environments requiring model transparency and auditability.\\
    
We develop a comprehensive evaluation protocol incorporating forecasting metrics (MSE, MAE, log-likelihood), distributional diagnostics (Wasserstein distance, Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistics, tail indices), stylized fact preservation (volatility clustering, leverage effects), stress testing (crisis-period performance), and statistical significance testing (Wilcoxon signed-rank tests). This multifaceted approach provides a more complete assessment of model performance than typical single-metric evaluations.\\
    
We provide evidence that unconditional (state-invariant) innovation distributions, while simpler and more interpretable, may be insufficient for capturing regime-dependent tail behaviour and time-varying asymmetry, particularly in foreign exchange markets and during abrupt volatility shocks. This clarifies the limits of two-stage architectures and motivates conditional flow extensions as a direction for future research.\\

We document systematic differences in NF-GARCH performance across model specifications. The per-asset forecast table and Wilcoxon tests show that gjrGARCH and one sGARCH specification achieve statistically significant improvements; TGARCH and the other sGARCH show mixed or nonsignificant results. This heterogeneity informs model- and asset-specific selection strategies.\\
    
Through stress testing across the 2008 GFC and 2020 COVID-19 windows, we find that NF-GARCH and Standard GARCH achieve similar forecast errors; neither systematically dominates in these stress regimes.\\
    
We demonstrate that NF-GARCH improvements are most pronounced for asset--model pairs where the baseline is Gaussian or symmetric Student-$t$; the per-asset table and Wilcoxon tests show significant gains for gjrGARCH and one sGARCH specification. Even with skewed-t baselines, flow augmentation improves forecasts on several assets, indicating that distributional flexibility provides value beyond what advanced parametric families can capture.\\
    
We explicitly address computational costs, interpretability trade-offs, and integration with existing risk management infrastructure. The modular two-stage design enables incremental adoption—institutions can enhance existing GARCH implementations by adding the NF component without requiring full model replacement, lowering barriers to practical deployment.\\
    
In summary, this dissertation shows that innovation distribution flexibility—when isolated from volatility dynamics in a two-stage design—can yield statistically significant forecast improvements for some GARCH specifications (gjrGARCH, sGARCH with skewed-$t$) while preserving interpretability. Gains are not uniform: mean improvements are modest, and for sGARCH with normal innovations NF-GARCH does not improve on average. The framework clarifies both the potential and the limits of hybrid approaches and provides a basis for future work on conditional flows, multivariate extensions, and state-dependent innovation modelling.\\

\section{Volatility Modelling with ARCH and GARCH}
\subsection{ARCH and GARCH models in time series generation}
\noindent
\textcite{engle1982autoregressive} observed the variance of historical stock returns over a 20-year period and eventually developed a statistical model for volatility, known as Autoregressive Conditional Heteroscedasticity ("ARCH"), which utilises historical patterns of variance to predict future volatility. \textcite{engle1982autoregressive} used ARCH models to simultaneously model the mean and the variance of a time series process, as ARCH models allow the conditional variance to be an Autoregressive ("AR") process, where the attitude of investors towards expected returns, as well as towards risk and uncertainty, can be modelled. This innovation allowed for the modelling of time-varying variances which may be conditional on lagged effects (i.e., autocorrelation). \textcite{bollerslev1986generalised} extended \textcite{engle1982autoregressive}'s ARCH models by allowing the conditional variance to be modelled as an ARMA process. This extended process is known as the GARCH process.\\

Over the past four decades, numerous nonlinear models have been developed for modelling and forecasting exchange rates. \textcite{chang1999methodical} and \textcite{gencay1999linear} argue that the random walk model is a more effective predictor due to the significant unpredictability of exchange rates. However, since its development by \textcite{bollerslev1986generalised}, GARCH-type models have become the predominant choice in the financial industry for modelling exchange rate volatility. \textcite{ederington2005forecasting} conducted a study on forecasting volatility in equity prices within financial markets and found that GARCH(1,1) generally yields better forecasts than the historical standard deviation and exponentially weighted moving average models. As was noted by \textcite{ederington2005forecasting}, equity financial markets exhibit longer memory effects and more asymmetric leverage effects than what is captured by GARCH(1,1) model estimates, thus future research focused on these limitations to the GARCH model framework.\\

\textcite{epaphra2016modeling} used the exponential GARCH (EGARCH) model to capture the symmetry effect in the daily TZ/SUSD exchange rate data as well as the asymmetry in volatility clustering and the leverage effect in the exchange rate. \textcite{epaphra2016modeling} showed that past volatility significantly influences current volatility in exchange rates, and positive shocks tend to have a greater impact on future conditional variance than negative shocks of the same magnitude. \textcite{abdullah2020using} extended on \textcite{epaphra2016modeling}'s analysis by studying the exchange rate volatility of EUR/USD and GBP/USD using daily closing prices from January 2015 until May 2020, with all models having been selected based on the student's t-distribution, and showed that threshold GARCH (TGARCH) and EGARCH models consistently outperformed other GARCH models in modelling the major currency pairs. \\

Equity returns display pronounced leverage effects, whereby negative return innovations increase future conditional volatility to a greater extent than positive innovations of equal magnitude \parencite{black1976studies,christie1982stochastic,cont2001empirical}. Consequently, models that assume symmetric volatility responses to past shocks, such as the standard GARCH model, tend to misrepresent risk during market crashes and earnings surprises. \\

The Exponential GARCH model \parencite{nelson1991conditional} specifies the logarithm of the conditional variance at time $t$ and incorporates an asymmetric response through a function of the standardised innovation, thereby eliminating the need for positivity constraints on the variance dynamics and effectively capturing the empirical ``news impact curve'' observed in equity markets \parencite{engle1993measuring}. \\

The Threshold GARCH, also known as the TARCH model \parencite{zakoian1994threshold}, introduces an explicit indicator variable that distinguishes negative innovations from non-negative innovations, ensuring that adverse return shocks increase conditional volatility more than favourable shocks. This asymmetric specification aligns with the downside-risk and crash-risk characteristics of stock returns and improves the accuracy of tail-sensitive risk measures such as Value at Risk and Expected Shortfall \parencite{hansen1994autoregressive,bauwens2006multivariate}. \\

Empirical evidence indicates that these asymmetric GARCH specifications frequently outperform symmetric GARCH models for equity returns, as capital structure effects and volatility-feedback mechanisms amplify the influence of negative returns on future conditional variance \parencite{campbell1992no}. As such, TGARCH and EGARCH are widely recognised as advanced GARCH-type models for modelling equity and exchange rate volatility, particularly in settings where leverage effects and asymmetric responses to shocks are empirically significant.\\


%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
% Literature Review - Stylized Facts of Exchange Rate Data

\subsection{Stylized Facts of Exchange Rate Data}
\noindent 
Stylized facts refer to empirical patterns consistently observed across various datasets. Originally introduced by \textcite{cont2001empirical}, these generalised observations, though not tied to specific theoretical models, provide valuable insights into the underlying structure and dynamics of financial markets.\\

In the context of exchange rates, several key stylized facts have emerged from the analysis of exchange rate data. An analysis performed by \textcite{julio2015stylized} on high-frequency exchange rate returns revealed distributions with power-law tails, thereby suggesting departures from the assumption of normality in financial returns. Moreover, scaling laws and seasonality had been identified by \textcite{aloud2013stylized} in exchange rate transaction data, with consistent quantitative relationships observed across different currency pairs. Additionally, as noted by \textcite{julio2015stylized}, cyclic patterns and correlations in exchange rate data point to the influence of broader market factors, such as carry trade strategies and shifts in investor risk appetite, on exchange rate movements. Accordingly, these stylized facts are commonly used as qualitative benchmarks when evaluating the realism of volatility models and their residual distributions.\\

Building on the observations of \textcite{julio2015stylized} and \textcite{aloud2013stylized}, \textcite{cont2001empirical} identifies a set of stylised empirical regularities that are consistently observed across financial return series and present significant challenges to standard econometric models. Specifically, asset returns exhibit heavy-tailed distributions with excess kurtosis compared to the Gaussian benchmark, indicating a greater likelihood of extreme outcomes. Volatility dynamics are marked by pronounced clustering, where large price changes, regardless of direction, are typically followed by further large changes, reflecting persistent conditional heteroskedasticity. Return series also frequently display leverage effects, in which negative returns lead to larger subsequent increases in volatility than positive returns of similar magnitude. Although raw returns generally show minimal linear autocorrelation, strong and persistent autocorrelation is evident in squared or absolute returns, highlighting the limitations of constant-variance assumptions. Moreover, return distributions tend to approach Gaussianity as the sampling horizon lengthens, a phenomenon known as aggregational Gaussianity. These stylised facts collectively support the adoption of more flexible modelling frameworks for volatility and innovation processes, moving beyond classical linear and homoskedastic models.\\


%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
% Literature Review - Limitations of Parametric Innovations in GARCH Models

\subsection{Limitations of Parametric Innovations in GARCH Models}
\noindent 
Although widely used, GARCH-family models generally assume fixed parametric forms for the distribution of standardized residuals, most commonly Gaussian or Student-t. However, empirical return data often violate these assumptions by exhibiting skewness, heavy tails, and multi-modal patterns that simple parametric distributions cannot adequately represent. While extensions such as the skewed-t \parencite{hansen1994autoregressive,fernandez1998multivariate} or Generalized Error Distribution (GED) \parencite{nelson1991conditional} partially address these limitations, they remain restrictive and may not perform well under regime shifts or nonlinear dependencies.\\

Initial efforts to enhance the innovation process primarily focused on substituting alternative parametric distributions. For example, \textcite{bollerslev1987conditionally} proposed the Student-t distribution to account for excess kurtosis, while \textcite{hansen1994autoregressive} introduced the skewed-t distribution to address asymmetry. Later developments incorporated mixtures of normals \parencite{hu2005semiparametric} and generalised hyperbolic or stable distributions \parencite{mcneil2000estimation,bauwens2006multivariate}, which provided increased tail flexibility but often resulted in additional parameters and reduced model tractability.\\

Semi- and nonparametric approaches have been developed to estimate the residual density directly. \textcite{engle1993measuring} and \textcite{ghysels1996filtering} explored kernel-based and adaptive likelihood methods for modelling conditional densities, while \textcite{jones2003nonparametric} and \textcite{hansen2004nonparametric} utilised local-likelihood techniques to estimate time-varying innovation distributions. Despite achieving improved empirical fit, these methods are often characterised by high variance and computational instability, especially in high-dimensional or heavy-tailed contexts.\\

Recent research has increasingly adopted machine-learning-based density models to relax or eliminate rigid parametric assumptions in financial time series modelling. Neural network-based conditional volatility and density models have been introduced through mixture density networks \parencite{bishop1994mixture} and deep neural conditional density estimators, including autoregressive and flow-based architectures \parencite{papamakarios2017masked,papamakarios2021normalizing}. Nevertheless, the majority of machine-learning applications in volatility modelling have focused primarily on volatility forecasting or conditional mean and variance prediction, rather than on explicitly redefining the innovation distribution itself \parencite{bao2017deep}. \\

Only a limited number of studies have directly replaced the residual distributions of GARCH-family models with flexible generative density estimators \parencite{seitz2022garchnf,wu2022deepgarch}. In this context, Normalising Flows constitute one of the first tractable modelling approaches capable of learning highly flexible innovation distributions while maintaining exact likelihood evaluation and preserving the interpretability of the classical GARCH volatility recursion \parencite{rezende2015variational,papamakarios2021normalizing}.\\


\textcite{bauwens2006multivariate} and \textcite{hansen1994autoregressive} highlight that the choice of innovation distribution is critical for accurately capturing Value at Risk, Expected Shortfall, and other tail-sensitive risk measures. Previous efforts employing skewed or fat-tailed parametric forms \parencite{bollerslev1987conditionally,hansen1994autoregressive} or nonparametric resampling \parencite{mcneil2000estimation} have only partially addressed these challenges. In contrast, data-driven density models such as Normalising Flows are capable of learning complex, multimodal residual structures directly from empirical data while preserving the structural interpretability of traditional GARCH frameworks.\\

Collectively, these approaches delineate a spectrum of residual-modelling strategies. Mixture distributions and heavy-tailed parametric families enhance tail behaviour but remain globally parametric and are often challenging to calibrate \parencite{bauwens2006multivariate}. Kernel and local-likelihood methods further relax parametric assumptions, yet they exhibit sensitivity to bandwidth selection, high variance, and limited scalability for extended or high-frequency financial time series \parencite{silverman1986density}. Machine-learning-based conditional density models offer increased flexibility; however, most existing work emphasises volatility or mean prediction rather than explicitly redefining the innovation law while preserving tractable likelihoods and the standard GARCH recursion \parencite{bao2017deep}. In this context, Normalising Flows represent a distinctive intermediate approach: they are parametrically specified yet highly flexible in practice, retain exact likelihoods, and enable efficient sampling. Although the flexibility of Normalising Flows depends on the choice of base distribution and transformation class, empirical evidence suggests that suitably parameterised flow architectures offer a practical and tractable approximation to innovation distributions observed in financial time series \parencite{papamakarios2021normalizing}, thereby positioning Normalising Flows as promising candidates for semi-parametric innovation modelling within GARCH frameworks.\\


%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
% Literature Review - Normalising Flows within Financial Time Series and Volatility Modelling

\subsection{Normalising Flows within Financial Time Series and Volatility Modelling}
\noindent 
Normalising Flows belong to the broader class of \textit{generative models}, which aim to learn data distributions by transforming simple latent variables into complex observations. Like Generative Adversarial Networks ("GANs") and Variational Autoencoders ("VAEs"), NFs approximate high-dimensional probability densities; however, unlike these models, they provide exact likelihood computation through invertible transformations, making them particularly suitable for financial modelling where density estimation is essential \parencite{rezende2015variational}. This is achieved by  transforming simple data distributions (e.g., standard normal) into more complex distributions through a sequence of invertible and differentiable functions, allowing for efficient sampling and density evaluation. Initially introduced by \textcite{rezende2015variational}, these models preserve the latent structure of data, making them versatile for various applications, including variational inference, density estimation, and anomaly detection. As a powerful tool for creating flexible probability distributions, \textcite{kobyzev2020normalising} showed that Normalising Flows have potential applications across multiple domains, highlighting their ability to produce tractable distributions with efficient sampling and density evaluation as well as enabling interpretable control over latent structures.\\

Recent research has compared the performance of financial time-series-based Generative Adversarial Networks against Normalising Flows for density estimation. \textcite{liu2020flows} found that Wasserstein GAN ("WGAN") struggled with modelling simple low-dimensional data, whilst the Normalising Flow-based models excelled at these tasks, outperforming WGAN in both the qualitative and quantitative metrics. Despite this, \textcite{liu2020flows} noted that the results related to the low-dimensional data could not be generalised to the higher-dimensional data settings as the sampled data generated low variance (and unbiased) estimates of the Wasserstein-1 distance, which impeded the recursive learning process of the generator, thus resulting in mode collapse.  \\

\textcite{dasgupta2022flowgan} proposed a novel approach called Flow-GAN, which combines GANs and Normalising Flows for solving high-dimensional Bayesian inverse problems. This method leverages GANs for dimension reduction and prior definition, and uses Normalising Flows for variational Bayesian inference by incorporating a Normalising Flow into the generator of the FlowGAN model, thereby transforming a prior noise density into a model density through a sequence of invertible transformations. \textcite{dasgupta2022flowgan}'s model showed that combining GANs and Normalising Flows allows for a more accurate approximation of the posterior, overcoming the typical challenges associated with high-dimensional inverse problems, such as capturing complex, non-Gaussian posterior distributions and ensuring computational feasibility in large-scale applications.\\

While most flow-based work has focused on computer vision and NLP, recent studies have begun applying NFs to financial time series. \textcite{liu2020flows} showed that Normalising Flows outperform GAN-based models in low-dimensional density estimation tasks, especially when exact likelihood estimation is needed. \textcite{amini2020deep} explored the use of flows in risk-sensitive modelling and outlier detection in finance.\\

The integration of Normalising Flows into volatility models, particularly the GARCH framework, represents an emerging area of research. Replacing the residual distribution in a GARCH model with a trained Normalising Flow allows the innovation process to adapt more flexibly to empirical data, capturing skewness and heavy tails without imposing a fixed parametric form. In this dissertation, these hybrid models are referred to as NF-GARCH models. These models retain the autoregressive structure of GARCH while enabling nonparametric learning of innovations (residuals). This methodology is anticipated to improve simulation quality, forecasting accuracy, and risk estimation, especially in stress scenarios.\\

\section{Modelling Review}
\noindent 
The theoretical review provides definitions and mathematical explanations of the key concepts relevant to this study, including ARCH and GARCH modelling, as well as the integration of Normalising Flows into GARCH models to enable flexible modelling of residual distributions in financial time series.\\

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
% Theoretical Review - ARCH and GARCH Modelling

\subsection{ARCH and GARCH Modelling}
\noindent
A time series comprises observations recorded at discrete, equally spaced intervals. It is typically modelled as a stochastic process, with each observation influenced by both deterministic and random components, thereby capturing the uncertainty inherent in real-world phenomena. Time series analysis seeks to identify temporal patterns or dependencies to facilitate predictive modelling. Forecasting involves sequentially indexed data points, emphasising the analysis of temporal relationships and patterns. This methodology is essential for anticipating financial trends and developing strategies to mitigate significant financial risks. A key feature in such analyses is heteroskedasticity, in which the variance of disturbances depends on the magnitude of prior disturbances, leading to fluctuating conditional variance over time.\\

A time series \({Y_t}\) can be considered as a discrete-time, continuous state process where \(t: t=l, 2, .., T\) are specific discrete points that are usually equally spaced intervals. A time series typically represents the progression of events over time and is often conceptualised as a stochastic process. Each successive observation in the series is viewed as a sample drawn from an infinite population generated by an underlying process. The core of a single-equation ARMA model lies in its ability to express the linear relationship between a given value in the series and its preceding values. In theory, once the "correct" model is identified, ARMA forecasts are considered optimal, as detailed in works by \textcite{BoxJenkins1976} and \textcite{pankratz1991forecasting}.\\

The foundation of standard ARMA analysis relies on the assumption that the mean and unconditional variance of the time series remain constant over time, implying stationarity. Techniques such as plotting the Autocorrelation Function (ACF) and Partial Autocorrelation Function (PACF), alongside tests like the Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) test, are employed to ascertain stationarity. For non-stationary time series, transformations are applied to achieve stationarity, as detailed by \textcite{pankratz1991forecasting}.\\

In addressing the challenge of high volatility in forecasting financial time series, recent developments have led to adopting models like GARCH. In GARCH processes, volatility (i.e., the variance of disturbances) is explicitly modelled. Tests for stationarity and other diagnostics closely parallel those used in ARMA analysis. \\

\textcite{engle1982autoregressive} showed that the serial correlation in squared returns, or conditional heteroskedasticity, can be modelled using an autoregressive conditional heteroskedasticity (ARCH) model of the form:

\begin{equation}
Y_t = \E_{t-1}[Y_t] + \epsilon_t
\end{equation}
\vspace{8pt}
\begin{equation}
\epsilon_t = \sigma_t z_t
\end{equation}
\vspace{8pt}
\begin{equation}
\sigma_t^2 = a_0 + a_1 \epsilon_{t-1}^2 + a_2 \epsilon_{t-2}^2 + \dots + a_q \epsilon_{t-q}^2    
\end{equation}
where \(\E_{t-1}[\cdot]\) represents the conditional expectation on all information that is available at time \(t-1\) and \(\epsilon_t\) is modelled as the product of a standardised shock \(z_t\) and time-varying volatility \(\sigma_t\) such that \(z_t\) is a sequence of independent and identically distributed ("iid") random variables with mean zero and unit variance. In the ARCH model, \(z_t\) is assumed to be independent and identically distributed with a standard normal distribution. The restrictions \(a_0 > 0\)  and \(a_i >= 0\) \(i = 1,..,q\) are required for \(\sigma_t^2 > 0\) .\\

An important extension of the ARCH model proposed by \textcite{bollerslev1986generalised} replaces the AR (p) representation in (2.3) with an ARMA (p,q) formulation:

\begin{equation}
\sigma_t^2 = a_0 + \sum_{j=1}^p b_j \sigma_{t-j}^2 + \sum_{i=1}^q a_i \epsilon_{t-i}^2  
\end{equation}

, where the coefficients \(a_i\) \((i=0,..,q)\) and \(b_j\) \((j=1,..,p)\) are all assumed to be positive to ensure that the conditional variance \(\sigma_t^2\) is always positive. The model in (2.4) together with (2.1) - (2.2) is known as the generalised ARCH or GARCH (p,q) model. When \(p=0\), the GARCH model reduces to the ARCH model.\\

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
% Theoretical Review - TGARCH

\subsubsection{TGARCH}
\noindent 
The threshold GARCH (or TGARCH) model was proposed by \textcite{zakoian1994threshold} and is a variant of the GARCH model that accounts for asymmetric effects in the volatility of time series data. Unlike standard GARCH models, which assume that positive and negative shocks have the same impact on volatility, TGARCH explicitly models the asymmetry by allowing positive and negative shocks to affect volatility differently. This makes the model particularly useful for capturing leverage effects, where negative returns (e.g., exchange rate drops) often result in higher future volatility than positive returns of the same magnitude.\\

The TGARCH model defines the conditional \emph{scale} as a piecewise linear function that responds asymmetrically to past shocks. In this dissertation, the implemented TGARCH(1,1) specification is:

\begin{equation}
\sigma_t = \omega + \alpha |\epsilon_{t-1}| + \eta \mathbf{1}\{\epsilon_{t-1}<0\}|\epsilon_{t-1}| + \beta \sigma_{t-1}.
\end{equation}

This scale recursion differs from the more common variance recursion and is reported explicitly to match the empirical implementation. The scale specification was chosen to align with the \texttt{rugarch} implementation used in empirical estimation.\\

The TGARCH model distinguishes between the effects of positive and negative shocks:
\begin{itemize}
    \item If \( \gamma_{i} > 0 \), negative shocks (bad news) will have a larger effect on future volatility than positive shocks (good news) of the same magnitude, capturing the "leverage effect."
    \item If \( \gamma_{i} = 0 \), the model reduces to a standard GARCH model, where positive and negative shocks have the same impact on volatility.
\end{itemize}.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
% Theoretical Review - EGARCH

\subsubsection{EGARCH}
\noindent 
The EGARCH model was proposed by \textcite{nelson1991conditional} and specifies the conditional variance in logarithmic form. This transformation into the logarithmic scale offers significant advantages, notably that it inherently removes the need for imposing non-negative constraints on the model parameters, which is necessary in standard GARCH models to ensure the variance remains positive. The EGARCH model's flexibility allows it to capture asymmetric effects of past returns, where negative shocks to returns increase future volatility more than positive shocks of the same magnitude.\\

In the EGARCH model, the conditional variance, \(\sigma_t^2\), is an asymmetric function of lagged disturbances \(\epsilon_{t-i}\):\\

\begin{equation}
{\ln}(\sigma_t^2) =     
        a_0 +
        \sum_{j=1}^p b_j {\ln}(\sigma_{t-j}^2) +
        \sum_{i=1}^q a_i g( z_{t-i}) 
\end{equation}
where
\[
g(z_{t}) = {\theta} z_{t} + 
            {\gamma \bigl[{| z_{t}|} - 
            \E[| z_{t}|]\bigr]}
\]
\vspace{8pt}
\[
z_{t} = \frac{\epsilon_{t}}{\sigma_{t}}
\]

The coefficient \(\gamma\) of the second term in (2.6) is set to 1 (\(\gamma = 1\)) in our formulation, avoiding notational conflict with the GARCH coefficients \(b_j\) in the variance recursion. Note that \({\E{|z_{t}|} = (2 / {\pi}) ^{1/2}} \) if \({z_{t} {\sim} \rm{N}(0,1)}\).\\

In the NF-GARCH variants studied in this dissertation—including NF-sGARCH, NF-TGARCH, and NF-EGARCH—we assume that the residual \( z_t \) is modelled via a Normalising Flow \( z_t = f(u_t) \), where \( u_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1) \). This removes the need to impose a fixed parametric form (e.g., normal or t-distribution) for residuals and allows the model to learn a flexible, data-driven innovation distribution.\\

The properties of the EGARCH model are summarised as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The function \(g(z_t)\) is linear in \(z_t\) with slope coefficient \({\theta} + 1\) if \(z_t\) is positive while \(g(z_t)\) is linear in \(z_t\) with slope coefficient \({\theta} - 1\) if \(z_t\) is negative
    \item Suppose that \(\theta = 0\). Large innovations increase the conditional variance if \({| z_{t}|}- \E[| z_{t}|]\) > 0 as the impact of past shocks on current volatility becomes symmetric, meaning that the volatility responds equally to positive and negative shocks of the same magnitude. The asymmetric effect governed by \( \theta \) disappears, and now only the magnitude of the shock \( |z_t| \) matters.
    \item Suppose that \( \theta < 0 \). The innovation in variance, \( g(z_t) \), is positive if \( |z_t| > \mathbb{E}[|z_t|] \), meaning that large innovations increase the conditional variance. Additionally, negative innovations in returns \( \epsilon_t \), cause a stronger positive impact on the conditional variance when \( \theta \) is much less than zero, reflecting the leverage effect.
\end{enumerate}

% Theoretical Review - Normalising Flows

\subsection{Normalising Flows}
\noindent
Normalising Flows represent a robust framework for constructing intricate probability distributions by applying a series of invertible transformations \parencite{rezende2015variational}. The initial probability density undergoes a sequence of mappings by systematically employing the change of variables formula, yielding a tractable complex distribution. The term "Normalising Flow" aptly describes this process, as the density effectively "flows" through the sequence of transformations, culminating in a normalised probability distribution \parencite{rezende2015variational}.\\

Based on the initial framework developed by \textcite{rezende2015variational}, we consider a finite sequence of transformations where each mapping is invertible and smooth. If we let \( f \) denote such a mapping with its corresponding inverse \( f^{-1} \), then for a random variable \(\mathbf{X}\) with an initial density \( p_\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{x}) \), the transformed random variable \(\mathbf{Y} = f(\mathbf{X})\) follows a density:\\

\begin{equation}
p_\mathbf{Y}(\mathbf{y}) = p_\mathbf{X}\left(f^{-1}(\mathbf{y})\right) \left|\det \frac{\partial f^{-1}(\mathbf{y})}{\partial \mathbf{y}}\right|
\end{equation}

\vspace{15pt}

This relationship is derived by invoking the inverse function theorem, which governs the behaviour of Jacobians for invertible functions. By composing multiple such mappings, one can generate arbitrarily complex densities. Specifically, if a random variable \(\mathbf{X}\) with density \(p_\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{x})\) is subjected to a sequence of \(K\) transformations \(f_1, f_2, \dots, f_K\), the resulting density of the final variable \(\mathbf{Z}_K = f_K \circ \cdots \circ f_1(\mathbf{X})\) can be written in terms of the base variable and the Jacobians of the forward maps: \(p_{\mathbf{Z}_K}(\mathbf{z}_K) = p_\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{x}_0) \prod_{k=1}^{K} \left|\det J_{f_k}(\mathbf{z}_{k-1})\right|^{-1}\), where \(\mathbf{z}_0 = \mathbf{x}_0\) and \(J_{f_k}\) denotes the Jacobian of \(f_k\). Equivalently, in inverse form,\\

\begin{equation}
p_{\mathbf{Z}_K}(\mathbf{z}_K) = p_\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{x}) \prod_{k=1}^{K} \left|\det \frac{\partial f_k^{-1}(\mathbf{z}_k)}{\partial \mathbf{z}_k}\right|
\end{equation}

\vspace{15pt}

As suggested by \textcite{kobyzev2020normalising}, the trajectory traced by the sequence of transformed random variables \(\mathbf{Z}_k\), starting from the initial distribution \(p_\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{x})\), constitutes the flow. In contrast, the sequence of intermediate densities \(p_{\mathbf{Z}_k}(\mathbf{z}_k)\) defines the Normalising Flow.\\

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
% Theoretical Review - Normalising Flows in GARCH Residuals (NF-GARCH)

\subsection{Normalising Flows in GARCH Residuals (NF-GARCH)}
\noindent 
Traditional GARCH-type models, including sGARCH, EGARCH, and TGARCH, often assume that the residuals \( z_t \) follow a known parametric distribution such as Gaussian, Student-\( t \), or Generalised Error Distribution. However, empirical studies have shown that these fixed-distribution assumptions are often too restrictive to capture the true nature of financial return innovations, which can be skewed, heavy-tailed, or even multi-modal.\\

To address this, we replace the assumption \( z_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1) \) with:

\[
z_t = f(u_t), \quad u_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d)
\]

where \( f: \mathbb{R}^d \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^d \) is a sequence of invertible, differentiable transformations—i.e., a Normalising Flow. This transforms a simple base distribution (such as standard normal) into a rich, learned distribution for residuals \( z_t \), thereby enabling greater flexibility in capturing empirical data characteristics. While alternative heavy-tailed base distributions such as the Student-t could be considered for the base distribution, we adopt a standard normal base for tractability and comparability, with tail flexibility introduced through the learned flow transformations.\\

Given a base density \( p_U(u) \) and an invertible transformation \( z = f(u) \), the transformed density of \( z \) is given by the change-of-variables formula:

\[
p_Z(z) = p_U(f^{-1}(z)) \left| \det \left( \frac{\partial f^{-1}(z)}{\partial z} \right) \right|
\]

or, equivalently:

\[
\log p_Z(z) = \log p_U(u) - \sum_{i=1}^K \log \left| \det \left( \frac{\partial f_i}{\partial h_{i-1}} \right) \right|
\]

where \( f = f_K \circ \cdots \circ f_1 \), and \( h_i = f_i(h_{i-1}) \) with \( h_0 = u \). Popular choices for flows include RealNVP \parencite{dinh2016density}, Masked Autoregressive Flows (MAF) \parencite{papamakarios2017masked}, and Neural Spline Flows \parencite{durkan2019neural}. Although these flows are commonly constructed using a standard normal base distribution, heavier-tailed behaviour can be accommodated through sufficiently expressive transformations, and alternative base distributions could be considered as extensions.\\

\subsubsection{NF-GARCH Model Structure.} The NF-GARCH model can be represented as:

\begin{align}
Y_t &= \mu_t + \epsilon_t \\
\epsilon_t &= z_t \sigma_t, \quad z_t = f(u_t), \quad u_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1) \\
\sigma_t^2 &= \omega + \sum_{i=1}^q \alpha_i \epsilon_{t-i}^2 + \sum_{j=1}^p \beta_j \sigma_{t-j}^2
\end{align}

\vspace{15pt}

Figure 2.1 highlights the modular nature of the NF-GARCH framework: the Normalising Flow modifies only the innovation distribution, while the volatility recursion remains structurally identical to classical GARCH models.\\

% Theoretical Review - Identifiability, Overfitting, and Stability

\subsection{Theoretical Considerations: Identifiability, Overfitting, and Stability}
\noindent
Normalising Flows offer a flexible and tractable approach for modelling innovation distributions; however, their integration with GARCH-family volatility recursions introduces several important theoretical considerations.\\

\subsubsection{Identifiability.}
Within the NF-GARCH framework, the conditional variance $\sigma_t^2$ is determined by the GARCH recursion, while the flow $f_\theta(\cdot)$ transforms latent noise $u_t$ into residuals $z_t$. There is a potential for a confounding effect between the volatility parameters and the transformations learned by the flow as both components influence the scale and shape of the simulated return given that:

\[
r_t = \mu_t + \epsilon_t, \qquad \epsilon_t = \sigma_t z_t, \qquad z_t = f_\theta(u_t),
\]

If the flow is excessively flexible, it may absorb variation that would otherwise be attributed to the volatility recursion, leading to partial non-identifiability between scale parameters in $\sigma_t$ and in $f_\theta$. Formally, writing $r_t = \sigma_t f_\theta(u_t)$, if $f_\theta$ includes an arbitrary scaling component then $\sigma_t f_\theta(u_t) = (\sigma_t c) \tilde{f}_\theta(u_t)$ for some constant $c$, so scale can be reallocated between the volatility recursion and the flow, implying partial scale non-identifiability. This is an inherent limitation of combining highly expressive innovation laws with parametric volatility models.\\

\subsubsection{Overfitting in expressive flows.}
Normalising Flows with numerous layers or high-capacity coupling networks are susceptible to overfitting the empirical residual distribution, particularly when applied to short financial time series \parencite{papamakarios2021normalizing}. Since flows are trained by maximising likelihood, they may capture spurious high-frequency structure or noise instead of the underlying innovation law. In volatility modelling, such overfitting can distort tail behaviour, degrade out-of-sample forecast performance, and yield misleading risk measures. Therefore, controlling model capacity, such as by limiting flow depth or width and employing regularisation techniques, is essential in NF-GARCH design.\\

\subsubsection{Likelihood regularisation and numerical stability.}
Flow-based likelihoods require computation of the Jacobian log-determinant,
\[
\log p_Z(z) = \log p_U\big(f^{-1}_\theta(z)\big)
             + \log \Bigl|\det J_{f^{-1}_\theta}(z)\Bigr|,
\]
which can become numerically unstable if the transformations are too deep or poorly conditioned. Large positive or negative Jacobian terms can lead to exploding or vanishing log-densities and hinder convergence in optimisation \parencite{papamakarios2021normalizing}. Practical implementations often mitigate these issues through weight decay, spectral constraints, or penalties on extreme Jacobian values to keep the learned density well-behaved \parencite{papamakarios2021normalizing}.\\

\subsubsection{Sensitivity to flow depth and architecture.}
While deeper flows are theoretically more expressive, \parencite{papamakarios2021normalizing} confirmed that they also increase computational cost and may interact unfavourably with long-memory volatility dynamics. In practice, shallow or moderately deep flows often provide sufficient flexibility to capture skewness and heavy tails in the residuals, whereas very deep architectures yield diminishing returns and intensify identifiability and stability concerns\parencite{liu2020flows} . Consequently, parsimonious flow architectures are generally preferable when integrating flows into GARCH-type models.\\

\subsubsection{Implications for NF-GARCH.}
These considerations indicate that NF-GARCH models function as semi-parametric volatility models, with their advantages contingent upon achieving a careful balance between flexibility and control. The GARCH recursion maintains its interpretability, whereas the innovation law becomes a learned component that requires regularisation to prevent overfitting and instability. Thus, a theoretically robust NF-GARCH specification necessitates careful attention to both the volatility structure and the capacity and regularisation of the flow.\\

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
% Theoretical Review - Two-stage NF innovations vs.\ end-to-end flow-after-scaling

\subsection{Two-stage NF innovations vs.\ end-to-end flow-after-scaling}
\label{sec:nf-contrast}
\noindent
\textbf{Two-stage} In this framework, the Normalising Flow is trained \emph{after} the standard GARCH estimation stage, rather than being embedded directly into the volatility recursion. This modular approach separates the estimation of conditional volatility dynamics from the learning of the innovation distribution, allowing for a clearer interpretation of each component. Specifically, we model:\\

\[
r_t=\mu_t+\epsilon_t,\qquad \epsilon_t=\sigma_t z_t', \quad z_t' \sim p_{\text{NF}},
\]

with $\sigma_t^2$ following the usual GARCH recursion and $p_{\text{NF}}$ learned from the standardised residuals $\hat z_t=(r_t-\hat\mu_t)/\hat\sigma_t$. Estimation is modular: first fit a GARCH model under a standard innovation assumption (Gaussian or skew-$t$) to obtain 
$\hat\sigma_t$, then train a flow on $\{\hat z_t\}$. 
Forecasts and simulations draw $z_t' \sim p_{\text{NF}}$ 
and propagate through the same recursion.\\

\medskip
\noindent
\textbf{Joint (end-to-end) training.} 
An alternative modelling strategy, proposed by \textcite{seitz2022garchnf}, would estimate the GARCH volatility parameters and the Normalising Flow jointly within a single likelihood function, often referred to as a flow-after-scaling or end-to-end calibration approach. In such a framework, the innovations are modelled as:
\[
z_t = f_\phi(u_t), \quad u_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1), \qquad 
\epsilon_t = \sigma_t(\theta) z_t,
\]
where both $(\theta, \phi)$ are estimated simultaneously by maximising 
the joint log-likelihood 
$\sum_t \ell_t(\theta, \phi)$. 
This allows the flow to adapt to the evolving volatility state 
and enables fully data-driven conditional innovation laws, 
but it introduces a highly non-convex objective and greater computational cost.\\

While theoretically appealing, this approach introduces substantial practical and conceptual challenges. Joint calibration entangles the scale effects of the volatility recursion with the shape flexibility of the flow, leading to partial identifiability between $\sigma_t(\theta)$ and $f_\phi(\cdot)$. As a result, improvements in likelihood or forecasting accuracy cannot be uniquely attributed to enhanced volatility dynamics or improved innovation modelling. In addition, the resulting optimisation problem is highly non-convex and numerically unstable, particularly for asymmetric volatility specifications such as EGARCH.\\

For these reasons, joint calibration is not pursued empirically in this dissertation. Joint estimation was not pursued due to numerical instability and the need to isolate the marginal contribution of innovation modelling. Instead, a two-stage design is adopted to deliberately isolate the contribution of flexible innovation distributions while preserving the classical GARCH volatility structure. Nevertheless, joint flow--GARCH estimation remains an important and promising direction for future research, particularly in settings where conditional density calibration is prioritised over interpretability.\\

\medskip
\noindent
\textbf{Implication.}
Two-stage designs generally provide modularity and interpretability, as the volatility recursion is estimated separately from the innovation law, which can enhance training stability and preserve standard volatility mechanics. This contrasts with joint end-to-end training, which, in principle, can yield more accurate conditional density calibration but typically introduces greater computational complexity, a more challenging optimisation landscape, and additional sensitivity to model specification.\\

\subsubsection{Relationship to classical GARCH.}
The proposed NF-GARCH framework strictly generalises the classical GARCH model. In particular, standard GARCH with Gaussian innovations is recovered as a special case when the Normalising Flow reduces to the identity transformation,\\
\[
f(u) = u,
\]
so that
\[
z_t = f(u_t) = u_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1).
\]
Under this restriction, the NF-GARCH model collapses exactly to the conventional GARCH specification with Gaussian residuals. Hence, classical GARCH models lie on the boundary of the NF-GARCH model class.\\

It is important to note, however, that this nesting is conceptual rather than operational: in the two-stage framework adopted in this dissertation, the flow is not jointly estimated with the volatility recursion, and the identity mapping is not imposed during training. As such, standard GARCH is not recovered through estimation but represents a limiting case within the broader NF-GARCH modelling space.\\

\begin{enumerate}
    \item The data reflects daily closing prices from public financial data sources, captured at market close (4:00 PM Eastern Time) on each trading day.
    \item All non-trading days, including weekends, US federal holidays, and exchange closures, have been excluded from the dataset, resulting in a continuous trading day sequence.
\end{enumerate}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
% Methodology - Data Preparation and Performance Evaluation

\section{Data Preparation and Performance Evaluation} 

\subsection{Data Splitting}

\noindent
The dataset is divided chronologically, allocating 65\% to the in-sample period for model estimation and 35\% to the out-of-sample period for forecast evaluation. GARCH parameters are estimated using the in-sample data, and standardised residuals from this period are used to train the Normalising Flow model. The flow is trained exclusively on residuals from the training set to prevent look-ahead bias. Since the flow conducts unsupervised density estimation of the innovation distribution rather than predictive learning, training uses only in-sample residuals; the implementation may reserve a fraction of these residuals (e.g. 20\%) for validation-based early stopping, but the out-of-sample test set is never used for training or model selection. All model parameters are fixed before forecasting, and performance is assessed exclusively on the held-out test set.

\medskip
\noindent
To improve robustness, a rolling time-series cross-validation ("TSCV") framework with a fixed training window of 500 observations and a 20-step forecast horizon was implemented. For computational efficiency, only a small number of evenly spaced windows were evaluated per asset (typically three to four), rather than an exhaustive rolling scheme. This provides early–mid–late coverage while keeping total runtime tractable.

\medskip
\noindent
For each fold, the training set included observations from \(t_{\text{start}}\) to \(t_{\text{start}} + 499\), and the test set comprised the subsequent 20 observations. This strict chronological ordering prevented any future information from entering the model training process. Assets were required to have at least 520 valid observations to permit a complete training–testing pair, and only models that successfully converged during estimation were included in the final analysis. In the summary tables, any run with non-convergence or with MSE or MAE exceeding 10$\times$ the median for its source group (NF\_GARCH vs Standard) was excluded before computing mean statistics.

\medskip
\noindent
Performance metrics (including Mean Squared Error, Mean Absolute Error, Akaike Information Criterion, and Bayesian Information Criterion) were computed for each window independently and then summarised using mean values after applying the convergence and outlier filter described above. This design captures the temporal variability of model performance across multiple market regimes and ensures that reported averages reflect stable forecasting behaviour rather than a small number of unstable fits.\\

While return-forecast accuracy is assessed using MSE and MAE on one-step-ahead return predictions, the benefits of NF-GARCH are primarily evaluated through distributional diagnostics, tail-risk exceedance diagnostics, stress testing, and the replication of stylised facts, rather than through direct loss-based evaluation of conditional variance forecasts.\\

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
% Methodology - Exploratory Data Analysis

\subsection{Exploratory Data Analysis}
\noindent 
After data cleaning and transformation, an extensive exploratory data analysis was performed to uncover structure, anomalies, and financial features in the dataset. The exploratory analysis commenced with the calculation of descriptive summary statistics, including mean, variance, skewness, and kurtosis, to characterise the distributional properties of each series. Temporal trends and volatility dynamics were identified through visual inspection of line plots depicting price levels and log returns. \\

Cross-asset relationships were evaluated using correlation heatmaps and Pearson correlation matrices for both foreign exchange pairs and equity assets. Empirical stylized facts, such as volatility clustering, deviations from normality, and heavy-tailed behaviour, were assessed using graphical and statistical diagnostics. The stationarity of returns was subsequently verified using Augmented Dickey–Fuller tests, with autocorrelation and partial autocorrelation analyses providing additional confirmation of the suitability of the time-series model.\\

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
% Methodology - Model Training and Fitting

\section{Model Development and Implementation} 

\subsection{Model Training and Fitting}

\noindent
The designated training subsets of each asset series were used for model training, with subsequent testing and cross-validation conducted on holdout and rolling windows. All GARCH-family models were implemented manually in \textsf{R} using a custom-built estimation engine to maintain full control over parameterisation and diagnostics. Four variants were fitted separately for each asset: the standard GARCH model with normal and Student-$t$ innovations, the Exponential GARCH model to capture asymmetric effects, the Glosten–Jagannathan–Runkle GARCH model incorporating leverage terms, and the Threshold GARCH model accommodating regime-dependent volatility responses. Parameter estimation was performed using maximum likelihood optimisation via the \texttt{optim} function in \textsf{R}. Model adequacy was evaluated using standard information criteria (AIC and BIC), forecast error metrics (MSE and MAE), and residual diagnostics, including the Ljung–Box and ARCH–LM tests. Non-convergent fits were excluded from downstream summaries.\\

\medskip
\noindent
For the Normalising Flow component, standardised residuals from each fitted GARCH model were extracted and used to train an independent Masked Autoregressive Flow implemented with the \texttt{MaskedAffineAutoregressiveTransform} class from the \texttt{nflows} library in \textsf{Python}. Each flow comprised eight transformation layers with 256 hidden units per layer and was trained for 150 epochs using a learning rate of 0.001, a batch size of 512, and an early-stopping criterion with a patience of 15 epochs based on a 20\% validation split. After training, the flow-generated innovations were re-injected into the original GARCH volatility recursion to construct the hybrid NF-GARCH models for simulation and forecasting.\\

In the two-stage NF-GARCH framework adopted in this dissertation, the Normalising Flow is not included in the calibration of the GARCH volatility parameters. The volatility recursion is estimated first under a conventional parametric innovation assumption, after which the Flow is trained separately on the resulting standardised residuals. The learned Flow is then used exclusively for return simulation, forecasting, and density evaluation by generating alternative innovation draws that are propagated through the unchanged GARCH recursion. This design choice is intentional and serves to isolate the effect of innovation distribution flexibility while preserving the interpretability and stability of classical GARCH volatility dynamics.\\

Note that all models were tested in a univariate setting for each asset series separately. As such, potential extensions to the existing framework could explore the integration of multivariate models such as Multivariate GARCH \textcite{bollerslev1990modelling}, BEKK-MGARCH \textcite{engle1995multivariate} and Dynamic Conditional Correlation \textcite{engle2002dynamic} to capture joint dynamics across currencies while preserving temporal structure.\\

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
% Methodology - Hyperparameter Selection and Model Capacity Control

\subsection{Hyperparameter Selection and Model Capacity Control}
\label{subsec:hyperparams}
\noindent
Normalising Flows introduce additional architectural and optimisation hyperparameters that require careful selection to balance expressive power and training stability. In this study, hyperparameters were determined through a constrained sensitivity analysis, guided by validation likelihood, numerical behaviour, and computational feasibility, rather than an exhaustive search. For the Masked Autoregressive Flow model, a range of configurations was selected consisting of flows with four to eight coupling or autoregressive layers, hidden layers of width 64 to 256 units, a batch size of 512, and the Adam optimiser with a learning rate of $10^{-3}$, combined with early stopping after 15 epochs without improvement in the validation log-likelihood. These settings were chosen by varying each hyperparameter over a limited grid (for example, three to six layers, hidden widths between 32 and 128 units, learning rates in the range $[5 \times 10^{-4},\, 2 \times 10^{-3}]$
, and batch sizes between 256 and 1024) and monitoring the impact on out-of-sample performance and training stability. \\

Increasing flow depth or width generally resulted in only marginal improvements in in-sample likelihood, while causing more erratic Jacobian log-determinants and clearer indications of overfitting, particularly for shorter return series. To address this, flow depth was limited and an L2 weight decay penalty of $10^{-5}$ was applied. The resulting architectures represent a deliberate compromise between flexibility and robustness, consistent with recent flow-based applications in financial density modelling. \\

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
% Methodology - Residual Stationarity Diagnostics

\subsection{Residual Stationarity Diagnostics}
\label{subsec:resid_stationarity}
\noindent
The two-stage NF-GARCH pipeline is predicated on the assumption that the standardised residuals
\[
\hat{z}_t = \frac{r_t - \hat{\mu}_t}{\hat{\sigma}_t}
\]
obtained after GARCH filtering constitute a weakly stationary series appropriate for density estimation. To evaluate this assumption, residual diagnostics were conducted for each asset-model pair. These diagnostics comprised unit root testing using the Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) test, stationarity assessment via the KPSS test, analysis of autocorrelation functions for $\hat{z}_t$ and $\hat{z}_t^2$, and computation of Ljung-Box Q-statistics for both raw and squared residuals. Additionally, ARCH LM tests were performed to identify any remaining conditional heteroskedasticity.\\


%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
% Methodology - Conditional vs. Unconditional Innovation Modelling

\subsection{Conditional vs.\ Unconditional Innovation Modelling}
\label{subsec:conditional_vs_unconditional}
\noindent

A key implication of the two-stage NF-GARCH design is that the Normalising Flow specifies an unconditional distribution for the standardised residuals by construction. Specifically, the flow models a time-invariant innovation density through an invertible transformation of an independent and identically distributed base variable, and does not incorporate explicit temporal conditioning or lagged dependence. As a result, any potential conditional heterogeneity in financial innovations (such as that observed during periods of market stress) is not modelled directly within the flow component. Instead, all time variation in return dynamics is intentionally captured by the GARCH volatility recursion through the conditional scale parameter $\sigma_t$.\\ 

This separation of roles mirrors the classical GARCH framework, in which innovations are assumed to be independent and identically distributed according to a fixed parametric law, such as the Gaussian, Student-$t$, or Generalised Error Distribution. The NF-GARCH framework preserves this structural assumption while relaxing the parametric restriction on the innovation distribution, replacing it with a flexible, data-driven density estimated via Normalising Flows. In doing so, the model isolates the contribution of distributional flexibility from volatility dynamics, enabling a direct assessment of whether a more expressive innovation law improves empirical fit, risk calibration, and the replication of stylised features of financial returns.\\

To evaluate the empirical validity of using an unconditional flow, additional diagnostics were performed on the standardised residuals, including rolling-variance measures, structural break tests, and comparisons of residual distributions across subsamples. The results indicate that, although the underlying series exhibit time-varying conditional variance as anticipated, there is no substantial evidence of structural breaks in the residual distribution and only limited signs of instability in the fitted flows. These findings support the decision to model the innovation law as time-invariant within the NF-GARCH architecture. Nevertheless, it is recognised that fully conditional or state-dependent flows could potentially capture more complex dynamics. However, such models introduce further identifiability concerns and optimisation difficulties, and are therefore reserved for future research.\\

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
% Methodology - Model Sensitivity and Robustness Checks

\subsection{Model Sensitivity and Robustness Checks}
\label{subsec:sensitivity_robustness}
\noindent
The robustness of the NF-GARCH results was evaluated across both architectural and stochastic dimensions. Sensitivity to flow architecture was assessed by perturbing key hyperparameters near the selected baseline, including the number of layers, hidden width, and coupling transform type (affine or spline). These perturbations resulted in only modest changes in out-of-sample loss metrics, such as mean squared error, mean absolute error, and log-likelihood. The qualitative ranking between classical GARCH and NF-GARCH models remained consistent. In contrast, extremely deep or wide flows sometimes led to unstable Jacobian log-determinants and irregular tail behavior, which supports the decision to constrain model capacity as outlined in Section~\ref{subsec:hyperparams}. The same convergence and outlier-filtering rule (non-convergence or MSE/MAE > 10$\times$ the median within the source) was applied to all robustness summaries to ensure comparability across configurations.\\\\

Additionally, NF-GARCH models were re-estimated using multiple random initializations to evaluate sensitivity to the choice of seed. Performance variation across seeds was minor compared to the systematic differences between model classes and asset types. This finding indicates that the observed improvements of NF-augmented models for specific equities are unlikely to be artifacts of a particular initialization.\\

A potential concern with the baseline specification is that restricting the analysis to GARCH(1,1) models may artificially constrain the classical benchmark, potentially overstating the relative advantages of NF-GARCH. To address this concern, a robustness analysis was conducted in which the optimal GARCH order was selected for each asset-model-distribution combination using the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) on the training data, allowing orders $(p,q) \in \{(1,1), (2,1), (1,2), (2,2)\}$.\\

For each combination, all four order specifications were estimated on the training set, and the order minimising BIC was selected. The classical GARCH model with this selected order was then evaluated on the test set, and compared against an NF-GARCH specification using the same selected order. This approach ensures that both models are evaluated at their respective optimal specifications, providing a fair comparison that accounts for potential order misspecification in the baseline. The evaluation employs the same chronological split and performance metrics (MSE, MAE, log-likelihood) used in the main analysis, ensuring consistency across robustness checks and primary results.\\

The robustness analysis serves to validate that the main findings are not driven by an artificially constrained baseline specification. By allowing the classical benchmark to select its optimal order via information criteria, the comparison becomes more stringent, testing whether NF-GARCH advantages persist even when the classical model is given the opportunity to improve through order selection. Results from this analysis are presented in Section~\ref{subsec:results_order_robustness}.\\

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
% Methodology - Model Evaluation and Prediction

\section{Model Evaluation and Prediction} 
\noindent 
After time-series cross-validation, the final models were evaluated on a held-out test set. Performance was measured using quantitative metrics and the verification of stylized facts, with all summary statistics computed after removing non-convergent runs and extreme-error outliers as defined in Section~\ref{subsec:hyperparams}.\\

A dual evaluation framework was implemented to compare NF-GARCH models with standard GARCH variants. Synthetic residuals generated by Normalising Flows were first assessed using distributional diagnostics, including Kolmogorov-Smirnov distance, Wasserstein-1 distance (Earth Mover's Distance), tail index (Hill estimator), skewness, and kurtosis \parencite{theis2015note, xu2019modeling}. This ensured that the injected innovations replicated key empirical properties of real-world residuals.\\

Second, forecasting performance was evaluated using both a 65/35 chronological holdout split and rolling time-series cross-validation (\parencite{bergmeir2012use}), with metrics including Mean Squared Error, Mean Absolute Error, log-likelihood, Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). To assess the statistical significance of performance gains, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was applied across matched model-asset-window evaluations \parencite{demvsar2006statistical}. Forecast win-rates were also computed to provide interpretable relative performance summaries. This methodology follows current standards in volatility forecasting and model comparison literature \parencite{amisano2007comparing, ziel2016forecasting}, and thus supports robust and transparent conclusions about both generative fidelity and predictive superiority.\\

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
% Methodology - Quantitative Performance Assessment

\subsection{Quantitative Performance Assessment}

\noindent
Model performance was assessed using return-forecast accuracy metrics and distributional similarity measures to ensure a comprehensive evaluation of forecasting quality. The error-based metrics include Mean Squared Error, Mean Absolute Error, log-likelihood, Akaike Information Criterion, and Bayesian Information Criterion, each computed on the held-out or rolling test windows and summarised using the filtered means described above.\\

\medskip
\noindent
Beyond pointwise accuracy, distributional fidelity between model-generated and empirical residuals was evaluated in accordance with the recommendations of  \textcite{ericson2024deep}. Specifically, the Earth Mover’s Distance, also known as the Wasserstein-1 distance, and the Kolmogorov–Smirnov statistic were employed to quantify discrepancies in overall distributional shape and cumulative density alignment. These measures are sensitive to both location and tail differences, thereby indicating how closely synthetic innovations replicate the empirical data distribution.\\

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
% Methodology - Qualitative Performance Assessment

\subsection{Qualitative Performance Assessment}

\noindent
In addition to quantitative metrics, model evaluation assessed the extent to which each specification replicated the fundamental statistical characteristics, or stylized facts, of financial time series. Adopting the framework of \textcite{kobyzev2020normalising} and \textcite{eckerli2021generative}, five principal properties were examined: linear unpredictability, where raw returns display negligible autocorrelation; the presence of fat tails compared to the normal distribution; volatility clustering, in which periods of high volatility tend to succeed one another; gain–loss asymmetry, reflecting the empirical observation that large losses occur more frequently than comparably sized gains; and aggregational Gaussianity, where return distributions become increasingly normal over longer time horizons.\\

\medskip
\noindent
A suite of visual diagnostics was employed to assess these qualitative features. Histograms on linear and logarithmic scales facilitated comparison between empirical and model-generated return distributions. Autocorrelation and partial autocorrelation functions elucidated the temporal dependence structure of returns and squared returns. Plots of conditional volatility and rolling variance highlighted the extent to which the models captured volatility persistence and clustering. Collectively, these diagnostics provided a complementary perspective on the models’ ability to reproduce the stylized empirical behaviour observed in real financial data.\\

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
% Methodology - Stress Testing and Extreme Events

\section{Stress Testing and Extreme Events}
\noindent
Model robustness under high-impact events was evaluated using the stress-testing framework proposed by \textcite{fu2019time}, which assesses model behaviour during both historical and simulated crises. This dual methodology enables systematic analysis of volatility model responses to actual market shocks and to controlled, synthetic perturbations in the data.\\

\subsection{Definition of Extreme Scenarios}
\noindent
Two categories of stress scenarios were considered: historical crises and hypothetical shocks.
The historical analyses examine two major global disruptions: the Global Financial Crisis ("GFC") and the COVID-19 pandemic. These events were selected due to their contrasting volatility regimes and persistence patterns. The GFC window spans 1 September 2008 to 31 March 2009, representing a period of systemic and prolonged market stress. In contrast, the COVID-19 window spans 1 February to 30 April 2020, capturing a shorter but more abrupt shock phase.\\

In addition to these empirical events, five hypothetical scenarios were constructed to assess model sensitivity to controlled extremes. These scenarios comprise sudden price drops of 30\% and 50\% introduced at 70\% of the series length, volatility spikes of two and three times the prevailing volatility applied at the same temporal point, and a sustained negative mean shift of 0.01 from 60\% to 80\% of the series length to simulate a structural downward trend. Collectively, these scenarios evaluate both transient and persistent deviations from typical market dynamics.\\

\subsection{Injection of Synthetic Anomalies}
\noindent

Synthetic anomalies were introduced through a deterministic perturbation of the observed return series, following standard stress-testing and scenario-analysis practices in financial risk modelling \parencite{engle1993measuring}. Let $\{r_t\}_{t=1}^T$ denote the original return series. Anomalous scenarios were generated by applying predefined shock functions to $r_t$ over selected time indices, while leaving the remainder of the series unchanged.\\

Price-drop scenarios were implemented as single-period shocks, in which the return at a specified time index $t^\ast$—chosen at 70\% of the sample timeline—was modified by subtracting a fixed value from the original return, such that $r_{t^\ast}$ was replaced by $r_{t^\ast} - \delta$ with $\delta \in \{0.30, 0.50\}$. This approach induces sudden negative returns of fixed magnitude, simulating abrupt price declines. Volatility spike scenarios were generated by multiplicatively scaling returns at the same temporal location, such that $r_{t^\ast}$ was replaced by $k r_{t^\ast}$ with scaling factors $k \in \{2,3\}$, inducing abrupt increases in local variance consistent with volatility shock events.\\

Mean-shift anomalies were introduced as sustained structural disturbances, implemented by adding a constant negative drift of $-0.01$ to returns over a contiguous interval spanning 60\% to 80\% of the observation window. This design emulates prolonged adverse market conditions rather than isolated shocks. These perturbations were applied independently to assess model robustness under transient shocks, volatility bursts, and persistent regime shifts.\\

\subsection{Training and Testing Under Stress}
\noindent
For historical crisis scenarios, each model was trained exclusively on pre-crisis data (all observations prior to the onset date) and tested within the crisis interval, with a minimum of 500 pre-crisis observations per asset. This design isolates the model’s ability to generalise from stable conditions to high-volatility regimes.\\

In contrast, for hypothetical shock scenarios, models were not retrained. Instead, shocks were applied exogenously to the return series, and their statistical consequences were analysed descriptively. This distinction reflects the differing purposes of the two scenario types: empirical testing of forecasting stability versus controlled sensitivity analysis.\\

\subsection{Performance Evaluation}
\noindent
Model performance under stress was evaluated across both scenario categories. For each crisis and synthetic shock, descriptive indicators were computed, including mean return, volatility, and maximum drawdown. For hypothetical shocks, the percentage increase in volatility relative to baseline was also calculated.\\

During historical crises, forecasting accuracy was compared between standard GARCH and NF-GARCH models using Mean Squared Error and Mean Absolute Error. As in the main analysis, crisis-period summaries exclude non-convergent fits and extreme-error outliers (MSE/MAE > 10$\times$ the median within source) before computing mean comparisons. The relative performance gain of NF-GARCH was quantified as the percentage improvement in MSE, defined as\\

\medskip
\[
\text{MSE\_Improvement\_Pct} = \frac{\text{MSE}_{\text{Standard}} - \text{MSE}_{\text{NF}}}{\text{MSE}_{\text{Standard}}} \times 100.
\]
\vspace{8pt}

All four GARCH variants (sGARCH, EGARCH, TGARCH, and GJR-GARCH) were tested in both standard and NF-augmented forms. The evaluation was conducted in full pipeline mode across 13 assets: six FX pairs (EURUSD, GBPUSD, GBPCNY, USDZAR, GBPZAR, EURZAR) and seven equities (X, NVDA, MSFT, PG, CAT, WMT, AMZN).\\

This design provides a comprehensive assessment of each model’s resilience across asset classes and stress environments, offering insight into how flexible innovation distributions influence volatility forecasting under extreme market conditions.\\

\section{Flow Architecture Sensitivity}
\label{sec:flow_sensitivity}
\noindent
The robustness of the NF-GARCH findings with respect to the choice of Normalising Flow architecture was evaluated through a series of sensitivity experiments in which key architectural hyperparameters were systematically varied around the baseline configuration. The number of coupling or autoregressive layers ranged from three to six, hidden widths were set between 32 and 128 units, and both affine and spline coupling transforms were examined. For each configuration, models were re-estimated using the chronological split and assessed with the same out-of-sample MSE, MAE, and log-likelihood metrics described in Sections~\ref{sec:baseline_garch} and~\ref{sec:nf_forecast_results}.\\

Within this range of architectures, changes in median out-of-sample MSE for both equity and FX assets were modest, typically remaining within a few percentage points of the baseline flow. The qualitative ranking among model classes was consistent: NF-augmented TGARCH and gjrGARCH variants continued to demonstrate the most reliable improvements over their classical counterparts for equities, whereas results for FX assets were less conclusive. Although larger and deeper flows sometimes yielded marginally better in-sample likelihoods, these did not result in systematic improvements in forecasting performance and were associated with increased instability in Jacobian log-determinants and irregular tail behaviour. These findings support the adoption of relatively simple flow architectures in the main experiments and indicate that the observed NF-GARCH improvements are not attributable to a single, highly optimised architecture. A comprehensive summary of the tested configurations and performance statistics is provided in the hyperparameter sensitivity workbook in the supplementary repository.\\

% Experiments and Results - Distributional Realism and Stylized-Fact Diagnostics

\section{Distributional Realism and Stylized-Fact Diagnostics}
\label{sec:dist_realism}
\noindent
The NF-GARCH model enables a flexible, non-parametric mapping between latent and observed innovations, thereby capturing skewness, heavy tails, and other higher-order moments that traditional parametric assumptions may overlook. To evaluate distributional realism, we employ two complementary approaches: (1) comparing NF-generated residuals to Standard GARCH residuals to assess replication ability, and (2) comparing both NF-GARCH and Standard GARCH residuals to actual empirical test set residuals---a stronger test that evaluates how well each model captures the true distributional properties of out-of-sample data.\\

\subsection{Replication of Standard GARCH Residual Distributions}

\section{Simulation Quality and Stylized Facts in Synthetic Series}
\label{sec:simulation_quality}
\noindent
In addition to point forecasts and residual diagnostics, a critical criterion for evaluating volatility models is their capacity to generate synthetic return series that replicate the fundamental stylized facts observed in empirical data. To assess simulation quality, long-horizon paths were generated from both standard GARCH and NF-GARCH specifications for each asset, using the estimated parameters. For each simulated series, key distributional and dependence metrics were calculated and compared to their empirical counterparts. These metrics included skewness, kurtosis, tail indices, autocorrelation functions of returns and squared returns, and leverage measures based on return–volatility correlations.\\

For both equities and foreign exchange assets, all model families qualitatively reproduce heavy tails and volatility clustering; however, significant quantitative differences are observed. Classical GARCH models with Student-$t$ innovations generally match unconditional kurtosis but tend to underestimate asymmetry and, for certain assets, exhibit weaker clustering in squared-return autocorrelations at longer lags. In contrast, NF-GARCH variants produce simulated returns with skewness and tail indices that more closely align with empirical series, particularly for equities. Furthermore, the autocorrelation structure of squared returns under NF-GARCH more accurately reflects the empirical decay pattern, demonstrating improved replication of volatility clustering and persistence.\\

Leverage effects present greater challenges for accurate replication, as they depend on the interaction between the volatility recursion and the innovation distribution. For equities, NF-augmented asymmetric models (NF-TGARCH and NF-gjrGARCH) generate simulated series with more realistic negative return–volatility correlations compared to their parametric counterparts, thereby reducing the discrepancy between model-implied and empirical leverage statistics. For major foreign exchange pairs, where leverage effects are less pronounced in the data, differences between standard and NF-GARCH simulations remain minimal.\\

To evaluate stress-testing realism, additional simulations were conditioned on estimated parameters from the GFC 2008 and COVID-19 subsamples discussed in Section~\ref{sec:stress_results}. Under crisis calibrations, NF-GARCH models generate synthetic paths with more pronounced and persistent volatility bursts, as reflected in elevated and slowly decaying squared-return autocorrelations and thicker simulated tails. These features more closely mirror the empirical behaviour of returns during stress windows than simulations from purely parametric models, which tend to exhibit either under-powered tails or overly regular volatility dynamics. Summary statistics for empirical versus simulated stylized facts are reported in the supplementary simulation workbook, and they reinforce the conclusion that NF-GARCH improves the realism of synthetic data, particularly for equity assets and during extended high-volatility regimes.\\

\section{Summary of Key Findings}
\label{sec:key_findings}
\noindent
Taken together, the evidence suggests that the value of NF-GARCH is not uniform but turns on the choice of baseline specification and, to a lesser extent, the asset class. At the aggregate level (Table~\ref{tab:nf-vs-standard-overall}), NF-GARCH achieves slightly lower mean MSE and MAE than the standard models, with AIC and BIC effectively unchanged---consistent with a design in which the GARCH component is estimated once and the flow is trained on the same residuals. Where one looks for systematic gains, however, the picture is more nuanced. Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (Table~\ref{tab:wilcoxon-tests}) reject the null of no difference at the 5\% level only for gjrGARCH and sGARCH when the baseline innovation is skewed-$t$ (p = 0.0156 in both cases); for sGARCH with normal innovations and for TGARCH the same tests leave the null intact. Win rates (Table~\ref{tab:nf-win-rate}) tell a similar story---gjrGARCH-\texttt{sstd} and sGARCH-\texttt{sstd} win in every comparison, whereas sGARCH-norm wins in only a third of cases and TGARCH in about two-thirds. The by-model summary (Table~\ref{tab:nf-vs-standard-by-model}) and the breakdown by asset class (Table~\ref{tab:nf_assetclass}) reinforce the point: mean improvements are small where they exist, and averaging across equities and FX yields very similar MSE and MAE for NF-GARCH and Standard, so the gains that do appear are concentrated in particular model--distribution pairs rather than spread evenly.\\

\medskip
\noindent
On distributional grounds, the picture is a bit clearer. When the criterion is how closely model-based residuals match \emph{empirical} test-set residuals (rather than how well the flow replicates the standard GARCH residual distribution), NF-GARCH does better on average: it attains a lower Wasserstein distance in about two-thirds of the model--asset combinations and a 10.2\% reduction in mean Wasserstein distance (Table~\ref{tab:empirical-comparison-summary}). That said, the flow-generated innovations themselves display near-zero skewness (Table~\ref{tab:distributional-metrics}) even when trained on residuals that are clearly asymmetric, so the improvement in realism is largely in overall shape and tail behaviour rather than in the capture of skewness. VaR exceedance diagnostics (Table~\ref{tab:var-backtesting}) are in line with nominal coverage for the models considered. Under stress---the 2008 GFC and the 2020 COVID-19 windows (Tables~\ref{tab:crisis-forecast} and~\ref{tab:forecast-crisis-model})---NF-GARCH and Standard deliver very similar forecast errors; neither systematically outperforms the other in these regimes.\\

\medskip
\noindent
So the takeaway from this chapter is conditional rather than sweeping: the two-stage setup preserves the interpretability of the volatility recursion while allowing a more flexible innovation law, and in this sample it yields statistically significant forecast gains for gjrGARCH and sGARCH-\texttt{sstd}, with modest average effect sizes and high win rates for those specifications. For sGARCH-norm and TGARCH the support is weaker or mixed. The gains are therefore specification- and context-dependent, and one would not want to claim on the basis of these results that NF-GARCH dominates across the board.\\

\section{Forecasting Performance and Innovation Misspecification}
\label{sec:disc_forecasting}

\noindent
The results indicate that Normalising Flow–augmented GARCH models improve forecast accuracy for some specifications but not others. Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (Table~\ref{tab:wilcoxon-tests}) show statistically significant improvements for gjrGARCH-\texttt{sstd} and sGARCH-\texttt{sstd} (p = 0.0156), with win rates of 100\% in both cases (Table~\ref{tab:nf-win-rate}). For sGARCH-norm and TGARCH-\texttt{sstd}, the tests do not reach significance, and win rates are 33.3\% and 66.7\% respectively. The by-model summary (Table~\ref{tab:nf-vs-standard-by-model}) shows that mean MSE and MAE improvements are small for TGARCH, gjrGARCH, and sGARCH-\texttt{sstd}, and slightly negative for sGARCH-norm. The detailed asset-level table (Table~\ref{tab:detailed-nf-vs-standard}) reveals heterogeneity: gains are concentrated in certain asset--model pairs rather than uniformly.\\

\subsection{Comparison with Prior Literature}
\noindent
These findings align with prior work on innovation misspecification. \textcite{hansen1994autoregressive} showed that misspecification of the innovation distribution propagates into biased volatility estimates and forecast errors. Our results provide empirical support: where the baseline is restrictive (e.g. symmetric Student-$t$), flow-based innovations yield significant improvements for gjrGARCH and sGARCH-\texttt{sstd}; where the baseline is already flexible or the comparison is symmetric (sGARCH-norm), gains are absent or reversed on average. \textcite{bauwens2006multivariate} demonstrated that flexible innovation distributions improve Value-at-Risk forecasts in multivariate GARCH; our univariate results show that innovation flexibility can also improve return-forecast accuracy for selected specifications, with significance confirmed by nonparametric tests.\\

The variation in performance across assets and models echoes \textcite{andersen2001distribution}, who documented that equity returns exhibit more pronounced volatility dynamics than major FX pairs. In our sample, mean MSE and MAE by asset class (Table~\ref{tab:nf_assetclass}) are very similar for NF-GARCH and Standard, suggesting that the benefit of flows is most visible at the specification level (gjrGARCH, sGARCH-\texttt{sstd}) rather than as an average over assets.\\

Crucially, these improvements result from correcting the residual distribution \emph{without altering the volatility recursion}. This isolation of the innovation effect distinguishes our findings from hybrid deep learning approaches \parencite{bao2017deep, kim2018forecasting} where improvements cannot be attributed solely to innovation modelling versus changes in volatility dynamics. \textcite{harvey2022machine} argue that successful hybrid models must preserve interpretability while demonstrating robust performance; the modular NF-GARCH design explicitly satisfies this criterion.\\

\subsection{Statistical Significance and Robustness}
\noindent
The Wilcoxon signed-rank tests \parencite{demvsar2006statistical} (Table~\ref{tab:wilcoxon-tests}) confirm that paired MSE differences favouring NF-GARCH are statistically significant for gjrGARCH-\texttt{sstd} and sGARCH-\texttt{sstd} at the 5\% level. For sGARCH-norm and TGARCH-\texttt{sstd}, the null of no systematic difference cannot be rejected. With six assets per model, the number of pairs is small; the fact that two specifications achieve significance indicates that the improvement in those cases is unlikely to be due to chance. \textcite{hansen2005forecast} note that nonparametric tests with limited samples require sizeable effect sizes to reject the null; our results are consistent with meaningful gains for gjrGARCH and sGARCH-\texttt{sstd} and inconclusive evidence for the others.\\

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Comparison with Alternative Flexible Innovation Methods}
\label{sec:disc_alternatives}

\noindent
To contextualise NF-GARCH performance, it is instructive to compare our results with alternative approaches to flexible innovation modelling.\\


\subsection{Comparison with Alternative Approaches}
\noindent
To contextualise our results, we compare NF-GARCH with alternative flexible innovation methods. \textcite{fernandez1998multivariate} developed skewed parametric distributions to capture asymmetry within tractable forms. In our evaluation, gjrGARCH with skewed-$t$ innovations still benefits from flow augmentation: the Wilcoxon test is significant and the win rate is 100\% (Tables~\ref{tab:wilcoxon-tests} and~\ref{tab:nf-win-rate}), suggesting that data-driven density learning can capture residual features beyond what this parametric family provides. For sGARCH-norm, by contrast, NF-GARCH does not improve on average (negative mean MSE improvement, 33.3\% win rate), indicating that the value of flows is specification-dependent.\\

\textcite{engle1993measuring} applied kernel density estimation to GARCH residuals, offering flexibility without strong parametric assumptions but requiring careful bandwidth selection and suffering from curse-of-dimensionality issues \parencite{silverman1986density}. In contrast, normalizing flows combine nonparametric flexibility with parametric tractability through invertible transformations, eliminating manual tuning while maintaining exact likelihood evaluation.\\

Generative adversarial networks \parencite{goodfellow2014generative, wiese2020quant} have been explored for financial time series generation. While GANs produce realistic samples, they suffer from training instability and mode collapse \parencite{arjovsky2017wasserstein}, and lack the tractable likelihood evaluation necessary for GARCH parameter estimation and VaR calibration.\\

End-to-end deep learning approaches \parencite{bao2017deep, kim2018forecasting} bypass GARCH structures entirely. While achieving competitive forecast accuracy, they sacrifice interpretability—practitioners cannot extract volatility persistence parameters or leverage coefficients critical for risk management \parencite{rudin2019stop}. \textcite{harvey2022machine} emphasize that in regulated financial applications, "black-box" models face substantial adoption barriers. The modular NF-GARCH design preserves all standard GARCH diagnostics while adding innovation flexibility, making it more deployable than end-to-end neural approaches.\\


%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Why EGARCH Underperforms with Flow-Based Innovations}
\label{sec:disc_egarch}

\noindent
A central methodological finding of this study is the persistent underperformance of NF-EGARCH relative to TGARCH, GJR-GARCH, and sGARCH specifications. Rather than reflecting a purely numerical or optimisation issue, this behaviour reveals a structural incompatibility between highly flexible innovation distributions and volatility recursions that already embed strong asymmetry mechanisms.\\

\subsection{Structural Sources of Instability}
\noindent
First, EGARCH models variance on a logarithmic scale \parencite{nelson1991conditional}, amplifying noise in the residual sequence. Minor irregularities from the Normalising Flow, especially in tail regions where coupling layers are most expressive, are magnified by the exponential transformation, leading to unstable volatility recursion.\\

Second, both EGARCH and Normalising Flows are designed to capture skewness and asymmetry in returns. \textcite{nelson1991conditional} explicitly designed EGARCH's asymmetric response function to model leverage effects \parencite{black1976studies, campbell1992no}, while normalizing flows learn flexible, asymmetric transformations. When these features are modelled simultaneously within the volatility recursion and the innovation distribution, the likelihood surface becomes weakly identified, as multiple components compete to explain the same empirical regularities.\\

\subsection{Parameter Redundancy and Identifiability}
\noindent
This finding aligns with broader econometric research on model identifiability. \textcite{rothenberg1971identification} established a foundational theory demonstrating that models with redundant parameterisations suffer from weak identification and unstable estimation. Our results extend this to the intersection of volatility dynamics and innovation distributions.\\

The consistent instability of NF-EGARCH—evidenced by extreme forecast errors and convergence failures on several assets—suggests that identifiability issues arise not only from overparameterization within a single model component, but also from redundancy \emph{across} volatility and innovation specifications. \textcite{francq2010garch} note that GARCH parameter estimates become unstable when innovation distributions are severely misspecified. Our findings suggest a more nuanced relationship: when innovation distributions are \emph{too flexible} relative to volatility asymmetry, the model struggles to allocate explanatory power appropriately between components, leading to estimation failure.\\

\subsection{Practical Implications}
\noindent
For practitioners, these findings suggest that innovation flexibility should be paired with \emph{simpler, more symmetric} volatility recursions such as sGARCH or modest asymmetry specifications like GJR-GARCH. Combining aggressive asymmetry in both variance dynamics (EGARCH) and innovation distributions (normalising flows) risks instability and poor out-of-sample performance. This design principle—"asymmetry in one component, symmetry in the other"—represents a novel heuristic for hybrid econometric-ML model construction.\\

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Distributional Realism and the Behaviour of Residuals}

\noindent
In addition to forecasting accuracy, the NF component substantially improves distributional realism when evaluated against empirical test set residuals---a stronger test than comparing model residuals to each other. NF-GARCH residuals align more closely with empirical test set quantiles, achieving lower Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Wasserstein distances in 66.7\% of model-asset combinations compared to Standard GARCH. The 10.2\% reduction in average Wasserstein distance (from 0.1898 to 0.1705) indicates that NF-GARCH innovations more accurately replicate the empirical distribution of out-of-sample test data, particularly for equity assets and certain FX pairs.\\

Importantly, this enhancement is achieved without altering the volatility recursion. The GARCH component continues to model persistence, clustering, and conditional heteroskedasticity, while the NF learns the shape of the innovation law. This decomposition preserves interpretability and aligns with the view of \textcite{cont2001empirical} that volatility dynamics and residual distributional shape represent distinct features of financial time series. Accordingly, NF-GARCH should be interpreted not as a replacement for volatility modelling, but as a targeted refinement of the innovation law within an otherwise classical and interpretable econometric structure.\\

However, this modular separation also introduces limitations. First, the Normalising Flow models an unconditional innovation distribution. While this assumption is consistent with classical GARCH methodology and preserves interpretability, it prevents NF-GARCH from capturing state-dependent changes in skewness or tail thickness. Such conditional heterogeneity is known to arise in foreign exchange markets and during abrupt structural breaks, and its absence may partly explain the weaker performance of NF-GARCH during the COVID-19 shock and for certain FX assets.\\

Second, despite the overall improvement in distributional realism, NF-GARCH exhibits a notable weakness in capturing skewness. When comparing NF-generated innovations to Standard GARCH residuals from the training set, NF consistently produces near-zero skewness (0.004-0.018) despite training on residuals that exhibit substantial asymmetry (ranging from -1.418 to 0.458). When evaluated against empirical test-set residuals (per-asset, per-model metrics in the repository), the near-zero skewness in NF-generated innovations suggests that NF struggles to learn asymmetric transformations. Thus, while NF-GARCH improves overall distributional shape (as evidenced by lower Wasserstein distances in the disaggregated outputs), its ability to capture skewness remains limited, potentially constraining its effectiveness for assets with pronounced asymmetric behaviour.\\

Third, per-asset distributional outputs show that NF-GARCH does not uniformly outperform Standard GARCH on kurtosis or tail matching: NF-generated innovations can exhibit higher tail indices than Standard GARCH residuals in some asset--model combinations, indicating thinner tails than the training data. These limitations in skewness and tail capture highlight that while NF-GARCH provides meaningful improvements in overall distributional realism for many pairs, it does not uniformly outperform parametric alternatives across all distributional properties.\\

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Model Robustness and Market Regimes}

\noindent
The stress-testing results reveal a regime-dependent pattern in NF-GARCH performance. During the 2008 Global Financial Crisis, which was characterised by prolonged and persistent high volatility, NF-GARCH models delivered modest but consistent improvements relative to their classical counterparts. In such sustained turbulence, the flexibility of the Flow-based innovations enhances robustness without destabilising the volatility recursion.\\

In contrast, during the COVID-19 crisis, which was marked by abrupt shocks and rapid regime reversals, NF-GARCH performance was mixed. In these environments, shifts in volatility dominate innovation effects, and the two-stage NF-GARCH structure struggles to capture transitory asymmetries or nonstationary tail behaviour. This outcome is consistent with the observations of \textcite{engle2002dynamic}, who emphasise that conditional heteroskedasticity models face structural challenges during extreme nonstationary episodes. In these environments, volatility dynamics and innovation distributions evolve jointly and rapidly, violating the conditional separation underpinning the two-stage NF-GARCH framework.\\

Overall, the evidence suggests that NF-GARCH is most advantageous in markets where volatility evolves smoothly but innovations deviate significantly from parametric assumptions. Its benefits diminish when regime shifts alter both volatility and residual structure simultaneously.\\

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Computational Considerations and Model Engineering}

\noindent
Normalising Flows introduce moderate computational overhead due to backpropagation through multiple coupling layers and the calculation of Jacobian determinants. Although they are far less computationally intensive than deep generative models such as GANs or diffusion networks, NF-based estimation remains more costly than traditional GARCH fitting.\\

The two-stage modular architecture used in this study, which first estimates the GARCH model and then fits the Flow to standardised residuals, offers several advantages. It preserves interpretability, simplifies implementation, enhances training stability, and supports model governance in regulated financial environments. End-to-end flow-GARCH hybrids, while potentially more expressive, would introduce greater model risk, identifiability challenges, and optimisation complexity. These design choices are particularly relevant in regulated risk-management environments, where model transparency, stability, and auditability often outweigh marginal gains in expressive power.\\


%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Limitations and Future Directions}

\noindent
Several limitations merit discussion. First, unconditional Flow-based innovation modelling cannot capture time-varying skewness or tail dynamics. Conditional or regime-switching Flows, though more complex, may address this. Second, while residual stationarity tests support the framework, mild remaining autocorrelation and ARCH effects indicate imperfect GARCH filtering; hybrid volatility–density models could help. Third, the Flow architectures used here were intentionally shallow for stability. Deeper or autoregressive Flows may achieve sharper density fits but risk overfitting or numerical instability.\\

Among these limitations, the most consequential is the assumption of an unconditional innovation distribution, which appears particularly restrictive in foreign exchange markets and during abrupt regime shifts.\\

Finally, the present study focuses on forecasting and distributional realism; future work could evaluate scenario simulation quality more deeply, including long-horizon clustering behaviour, volatility-of-volatility effects, and structural break replication.\\

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Practical Implications}

\noindent
For practitioners, the results suggest that NF-GARCH offers a practical and interpretable enhancement to classical volatility models. The hybrid design is particularly effective for equity markets and stress-testing applications requiring realistic tail and skewness modelling. FX applications may require more sophisticated state-dependent Flows to fully capture innovation dynamics. Importantly, the two-stage structure enables easy integration with existing risk infrastructure, making NF-GARCH an attractive tool for institutions seeking to improve accuracy without sacrificing transparency or regulatory compliance.\\

Overall, the evidence demonstrates that innovation distribution flexibility can meaningfully enhance volatility modelling when distributional misspecification is a dominant source of error, but offers limited gains when volatility dynamics themselves drive forecasting performance. By explicitly separating economic volatility dynamics from statistical density estimation, the NF-GARCH framework clarifies both the potential and the limits of hybrid econometric--machine learning approaches in financial time series analysis.\\

\chapter{Limitations, Future Work and Conclusion}

\section{Overview}
\noindent
This chapter provides a critical reflection on the methodological, empirical, and computational limitations of the study and outlines directions for future research. Although the proposed NF-GARCH framework demonstrates that flexible, data-driven innovation distributions can improve the realism of volatility modelling, several underlying assumptions and practical constraints restrict its generalisability and statistical power.\\

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
% Limitations and Future Work - Key Limitations

\section{Key Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
\noindent
Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged.  
First, the use of Normalising Flows introduces substantial computational overhead compared to closed-form parametric innovations. Each training cycle requires iterative density estimation over residuals, which increases runtime and memory consumption. These demands constrain scalability in large asset universes and limit applicability in low-latency contexts such as real-time trading.\\

\medskip
\noindent
A second limitation involves sample efficiency. Normalising Flows require large and diverse residual datasets to learn stable transformations. When applied to short, illiquid, or structurally unstable time series, performance may deteriorate. In these scenarios, the flow may underfit the tails or yield unstable density estimates, thereby compromising model calibration.\\

\medskip
\noindent
The third issue pertains to model complexity. Incorporating flow-specific hyperparameters, such as the number of coupling layers, network depth, and hidden width, increases both model expressiveness and the difficulty of tuning. Although these settings enhance flexibility, they also elevate the risk of overfitting and complicate cross-validation, especially since hyperparameters were held fixed across assets to maintain comparability.\\

\medskip
\noindent
Fourth, the analysis is restricted to a univariate framework. This limitation reduces the ability to capture cross-asset dependencies or systemic volatility spillovers. Extending the NF–GARCH framework to multivariate or copula-based settings is challenging due to the exponential increase in joint density dimensionality.\\

\medskip
\noindent
Fifth, the adopted two-stage design assumes a state-invariant innovation shape. The learned residual distribution scales with $\sigma_t$ but does not evolve conditionally with volatility. As a result, the model cannot capture volatility-dependent skewness or kurtosis. End-to-end or conditional flow architectures could address this limitation by explicitly linking distributional shape to volatility state.\\

\medskip
\noindent
Sixth, the absence of joint likelihood optimisation constitutes an additional constraint. The modular estimation procedure, in which GARCH parameters are fitted first and the flow is trained subsequently, simplifies implementation but prevents joint optimisation of the complete likelihood. This separation may limit achievable performance in metrics such as log-likelihood, Continuous Ranked Probability Score, or Value-at-Risk coverage compared to fully integrated monotone-flow models.\\

\medskip
\noindent
A related theoretical limitation concerns \emph{partial identifiability} between the volatility recursion and the Flow transformation. In asymmetric specifications such as EGARCH and TGARCH, both components can attempt to capture skewness, leverage effects, or tail thickness. This overlap can make it difficult to disentangle whether a given feature is being modelled by the conditional variance or by the innovation distribution, and likely contributes to the instability and sensitivity observed in NF–EGARCH fits.\\

\medskip
\noindent
A further limitation concerns residual dependence and bias. The flow is trained on standardised residuals assumed to be independent and identically distributed, yet mild temporal autocorrelation may persist. Without explicit pre-whitening, the flow may inadvertently capture serial dependence rather than the intended innovation density, potentially biasing synthetic residual generation.\\

\medskip
\noindent
Additionally, the fixed chronological split and a limited rolling-window cross‑validation design reduce statistical power. The small number of evaluated windows per asset limits the sensitivity of nonparametric tests, such as the Wilcoxon signed‑rank test, potentially obscuring subtle but systematic differences between models.\\

\medskip
\noindent
A further limitation concerns NF's ability to capture skewness. Despite training on residuals that exhibit substantial asymmetry (ranging from -1.418 to 0.458 across different GARCH specifications), NF-generated innovations consistently exhibit near-zero skewness (0.004-0.018). When evaluated against empirical test-set residuals (per-asset, per-model skewness in the repository), the near-zero skewness in generated innovations indicates that NF struggles to learn asymmetric transformations. NF-GARCH may therefore be less effective for assets with pronounced asymmetric behaviour, despite improvements in overall distributional realism visible in the disaggregated Wasserstein and KS metrics.\\

\medskip
\noindent
Finally, occasional convergence instability was observed in NF–EGARCH configurations, reflecting the non-convexity of deep probabilistic optimisation under small-sample conditions. Although early stopping and convergence filters mitigated these effects, stability remains a practical concern for operational deployment.\\


\subsection*{Additional Considerations}
\noindent
Several contextual factors also warrant discussion. The dataset, which consists of 13 assets (six foreign exchange pairs and seven equities) from developed markets, introduces potential sampling bias. Extending the analysis to emerging-market assets, commodities, or cryptocurrencies would enable broader assessment across more volatile and heterogeneous regimes.\\

\medskip
\noindent
A second consideration is model selection bias. The comparative analysis was limited to four classical GARCH variants (sGARCH, EGARCH, GJR-GARCH, TGARCH) and a single flow type (MAF). Alternative innovation laws, such as skewed or regime-switching Student-$t$ distributions, could reduce or reverse the observed performance differences.\\

\medskip
\noindent
The sensitivity of results to flow architecture introduces potential hyperparameter bias. Model performance depends on depth, width, and activation choice. Although default configurations were fixed for consistency, optimal settings likely vary by asset and could affect relative performance rankings.\\

\medskip
\noindent
Minor evaluation leakage may also occur. Despite the use of chronological data splits, early-stage hyperparameter tuning might indirectly influence subsequent validation folds, which could modestly affect generalisation metrics.\\

\medskip
\noindent
Lastly, minor backend differences between the custom R manual‑engine implementation and the Python Normalising Flow pipeline could produce small numerical discrepancies in residual extraction or optimisation tolerances. While cross‑validation confirmed alignment in general behaviour, small numerical differences may persist and marginally influence comparative outcomes.\\

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
% Limitations and Future Work - Future Work Directions

\section{Future Work Directions}
\noindent
Several promising avenues exist for extending the present study, each with the potential to enhance the theoretical depth and empirical robustness of the NF–GARCH framework.\\

\medskip
\noindent
A logical progression involves developing a multivariate NF–GARCH architecture capable of modelling joint innovation densities across multiple assets. Integrating Normalising Flows within established multivariate volatility frameworks, such as MGARCH, BEKK, or DCC specifications, would enable explicit modelling of cross-asset spillovers and the propagation of systemic risk. In particular, embedding Neural Spline Flows within a DCC–GARCH setting offers a concrete avenue for constructing flexible, state-of-the-art multivariate dependence structures while retaining familiar econometric volatility dynamics.\\

\medskip
\noindent
A related direction involves the integration of copulas and flows. Embedding Normalising Flows within Copula–GARCH architectures would enable learnable and highly flexible copula densities, thereby improving the model’s capacity to capture complex tail dependencies and nonlinear co-movement structures observed in financial returns.\\

\medskip
\noindent
Another promising enhancement is the development of pseudo-joint NF–GARCH training schemes. Alternating between GARCH estimation, flow training on extracted residuals, and subsequent GARCH re-estimation using the flow’s log-density for $z_t$ may approximate full joint likelihood optimisation while maintaining computational tractability and numerical stability.\\

\medskip
\noindent
Further research should explore conditional and state-dependent flows, in which the parameters of the flow are explicitly conditioned on $\sigma_t$, realised volatility, or exogenous macroeconomic indicators. This design would allow the innovation distribution to adapt dynamically to changing market regimes and capture volatility-dependent shifts in skewness and kurtosis.\\

\medskip
\noindent
Additionally, integrating NF–GARCH structures within regime-switching or stochastic-volatility models presents a potential means of improving robustness to structural breaks, multimodal distributions, and abrupt changes in market conditions. These hybrid designs may yield more stable performance under nonstationary or crisis environments.\\

\medskip
\noindent
From an operational perspective, investigating real-time or online updating of Normalising Flows constitutes a necessary practical extension. Streaming or incremental training algorithms would enable continuous calibration of innovation distributions in live trading or risk-monitoring systems, thereby enhancing adaptability to evolving market dynamics.\\

\medskip
\noindent
Finally, future work should consider enhanced evaluation and calibration protocols. Incorporating Probability Integral Transform diagnostics, conditional coverage tests, and Continuous Ranked Probability Score metrics would provide a more rigorous assessment of density calibration. Similarly, adopting bootstrapped or overlapping-window cross-validation could increase the statistical power of model comparisons and improve the reliability of performance inference.\\

\section{Synthesis and Outlook}
\noindent
In summary, although NF-GARCH maintains the interpretability of traditional variance dynamics and enhances distributional flexibility, its current implementation is constrained by data scale, independence assumptions, and computational demands. Addressing these limitations through conditional, multivariate, and pseudo-joint extensions may position NF-based volatility models as a practical bridge between classical econometrics and modern generative modelling.\\

\section{Conclusion}
\noindent
This dissertation investigated whether the integration of Normalising Flows into classical GARCH frameworks provides statistically and practically significant improvements in modelling financial return volatility. Specifically, it examined whether a two-stage NF–GARCH design, in which the variance recursion remains parametric and the innovation distribution is learned nonparametrically, can enhance forecasting accuracy, distributional realism, and risk diagnostics compared to standard GARCH models with fixed innovation laws. The empirical analysis addressed this question through four main objectives: documenting stylized facts for selected equity and foreign exchange series, establishing baseline GARCH-family performance, implementing the NF–GARCH pipeline, and evaluating forecasting, distributional, and tail‑exceedance metrics under both normal and stressed conditions.\\

\medskip
\noindent
The findings indicate that integrating Normalising Flows into classical GARCH frameworks can improve forecasting accuracy and distributional realism for some specifications without altering the variance recursion. Wilcoxon tests confirm statistically significant improvements for gjrGARCH and sGARCH with skewed-$t$ innovations; for sGARCH with normal innovations and for TGARCH the evidence is mixed. Mean forecast gains are modest in the by-model summary, and stress-period performance is similar for NF-GARCH and Standard. The benefits are thus specification- and context-dependent rather than uniform.\\

\medskip
\noindent
The results support the view that flexibility in the innovation distribution, when combined with a fixed volatility recursion, can improve empirical fit in selected cases. Conceptually, the proposed framework demonstrates that a well-established conditional variance structure can be combined with a modern deep generative component in a manner that remains transparent, modular, and compatible with existing risk infrastructure. Consequently, NF–GARCH should be regarded as a flexible extension of classical volatility models, strengthening tail modelling and distributional fit while preserving the underlying econometric structure.\\

\medskip
\noindent
Future research may extend this architecture to multivariate and conditional flow settings, where state-dependent innovation laws could further enhance risk calibration and cross-asset consistency. Specifically, multivariate extensions such as DCC or MGARCH with flow-based copulas, conditional flows with weak state dependence on $\sigma_t$, and comprehensive conditional-coverage Value-at-Risk  tests and Continuous Ranked Probability Score benchmarking represent logical next steps. Collectively, these directions have the potential to advance NF–GARCH from a promising hybrid prototype to a mature class of models at the intersection of econometrics and deep generative modelling.\\
